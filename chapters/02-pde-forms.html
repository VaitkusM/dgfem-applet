2Strong, weak, mixed & flux forms

One PDE, several ways to write it down — and each way leads to a different family of numerical methods.

A PDE written as "this expression equals that one at every point" is called the strong form. Almost every modern discretisation starts instead from an integral statement obtained by multiplying with test functions and integrating by parts: the weak form. Which quantities we keep as unknowns (only $u$, or also its flux) and where we integrate by parts (over the whole domain, or cell by cell) decides whether we end up with continuous finite elements, mixed methods, finite volumes or discontinuous Galerkin.

The strong form

Our model elliptic problem is the Poisson equation with mixed boundary conditions:

$$-\Delta u = f \;\text{ in } \Omega,\qquad u = g \;\text{ on } \Gamma_D,\qquad \partial_n u = g_N \;\text{ on } \Gamma_N .$$

Read physically: $u$ is a steady temperature, $f$ a heat source, the temperature is fixed on $\Gamma_D$ and the heat flux through $\Gamma_N$ is prescribed ($g_N = 0$: insulated wall). A classical solution must have two continuous derivatives so that $\Delta u$ makes sense at every point. This is a strong requirement: a heat source that is switched on in a square and off outside it, or a domain with a re-entrant corner, already produces solutions whose second derivatives do not exist everywhere.

The most direct discretisation of the strong form is the finite difference method: sample $u$ at grid points and replace derivatives by difference quotients, e.g. $-u''(x_i)\approx(-u_{i-1}+2u_i-u_{i+1})/h^2$ (chapter 0). Simple and effective on boxes; awkward on curved geometries, with variable coefficients or with discontinuous data.

The weak form

Instead of demanding the equation at every point, we demand that its residual is "orthogonal" to every test function: we only look at the equation through averages. Step through the derivation:

The natural home of weak solutions is the Sobolev space $H^1(\Omega)$ of functions whose values and first derivatives are square-integrable. Intuitively: functions with finite "stretching energy" $\int|\nabla u|^2$. Continuous functions with kinks (like tents) are in $H^1$, functions with jumps are not (their derivative would contain a Dirac spike with infinite energy). This explains why continuous finite elements must be continuous across element boundaries, and why discontinuous Galerkin methods (chapter 8) need extra terms to compensate.

Weak form = energy minimisation

For our symmetric, positive problem the weak form is equivalent to a minimisation principle (the Dirichlet principle): $u$ minimises the energy

$$J(v) = \frac12\int_\Omega |\nabla v|^2 \dd\mathbf x - \int_\Omega f\,v\dd\mathbf x$$

over all $v$ with the right boundary values. (Setting the derivative of $J(u + \varepsilon w)$ with respect to $\varepsilon$ to zero at $\varepsilon=0$ gives exactly $a(u,w) = \ell(w)$.) Physically: a membrane under a load $f$ settles into the shape of least energy.

Graphics analogy

Minimising $\frac12\int|\nabla v|^2$ subject to boundary values gives harmonic interpolation — the basis of harmonic mesh parameterisation, Laplacian mesh editing, Poisson image editing and diffusion curves. You have probably solved weak forms already without calling them that.

The Galerkin method

The weak form still involves infinitely many test functions. The Galerkin method replaces the function space by a finite-dimensional one, $V_h = \mathrm{span}\{\varphi_1,\dots,\varphi_n\}$, and asks for

$$u_h = \sum_{j=1}^n U_j\varphi_j \in V_h \quad\text{with}\quad a(u_h, \varphi_i) = \ell(\varphi_i)\quad (i = 1,\dots,n).$$

By linearity of $a$ in its first argument this is a linear system $A\mathbf U = \mathbf b$ with

$$A_{ij} = a(\varphi_j,\varphi_i) = \int_\Omega\nabla\varphi_j\cdot\nabla\varphi_i\dd\mathbf x ,\qquad b_i = \ell(\varphi_i).$$

If each $\varphi_i$ is nonzero only on a few mesh cells ("local support"), most $A_{ij}$ vanish: the matrix is sparse. In 1D with "hat" functions — $\varphi_i(x_i) = 1$, linear down to 0 at the neighbouring nodes — we get $A = \frac1h\,\mathrm{tridiag}(-1, 2, -1)$, the same matrix as finite differences (up to the factor $h$ and the right-hand side, which is now a weighted average of $f$).

Two properties make Galerkin methods attractive:

  • Galerkin orthogonality: subtracting the discrete from the continuous equations gives $a(u - u_h, v_h) = 0$ for all $v_h\in V_h$: the error is orthogonal to the discrete space in the "energy inner product" $a(\cdot,\cdot)$.
  • Best approximation (Céa's lemma): consequently $u_h$ is the best approximation of $u$ in $V_h$ measured in the energy norm $\sqrt{a(e,e)} = \|\nabla e\|$. The error of the method is the error of the best possible fit — approximation theory (chapter 0's interpolation estimates) gives the convergence rates.

The mixed form: the flux as an unknown

Often the quantity of interest is not $u$ but its flux $\boldsymbol\sigma = -\nabla u$ (heat flux, Darcy velocity in porous media, electric current). Differentiating a computed $u_h$ loses accuracy, so why not compute $\boldsymbol\sigma$ directly? Write the Poisson equation as a first-order system:

$$\boldsymbol\sigma + \nabla u = 0,\qquad \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma = f \quad\text{in }\Omega .$$

Multiply the first equation by a vector test function $\boldsymbol\tau$, the second by a scalar $v$, integrate, and integrate by parts in the first one ($\int\nabla u\cdot\boldsymbol\tau = \int_{\partial\Omega}u\,\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\mathbf n - \int u\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\tau$):

$$\begin{aligned} \int_\Omega \boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\boldsymbol\tau \dd\mathbf x - \int_\Omega u\,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\tau \dd\mathbf x &= -\int_{\partial\Omega} g\,\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\mathbf n \dd s &&\text{for all } \boldsymbol\tau,\\ \int_\Omega (\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\, v \dd\mathbf x &= \int_\Omega f\,v \dd\mathbf x &&\text{for all } v. \end{aligned}$$

(Here we took $\Gamma_D = \partial\Omega$.) Three remarkable changes compared to the primal weak form:

  • The roles of the boundary conditions swap: the Dirichlet value $g$ now appears in a boundary integral (natural), while a Neumann condition would prescribe $\boldsymbol\sigma\cdot\mathbf n$ and become essential.
  • $u$ appears without derivatives — it may be discontinuous (piecewise constant!), while $\boldsymbol\sigma$ needs a well-defined divergence: its normal component must be continuous across cell faces (the space $H(\mathrm{div})$).
  • Choosing $v$ = indicator function of one cell in the second equation gives $\int_{\partial K}\boldsymbol\sigma_h\cdot\mathbf n = \int_K f$: the discrete flux balances the source exactly in every cell, just like a finite volume method (chapter 5).

The price: the linear system has the block form $\begin{pmatrix} M & B^T\\ B & 0\end{pmatrix}$ — a saddle-point problem, symmetric but indefinite, and the two discrete spaces must be compatible (the inf–sup condition). Chapter 9 makes this practical with Raviart–Thomas elements and hybridization.

The flux form and cell-wise integration: FV and DG

For a conservation law $\partial_t u + \nabla\cdot\mathbf F(u) = 0$ (chapter 1) we can integrate by parts on each cell $K$ separately instead of over the whole domain:

$$\int_K \partial_t u\, v \dd\mathbf x - \int_K \mathbf F(u)\cdot\nabla v \dd\mathbf x + \int_{\partial K} \hat F\, v \dd s = 0 .$$

The boundary integral now runs over the cell's faces, where the neighbouring cell may hold a different value of $u$ — so the physical normal flux is replaced by a numerical flux $\hat F(u^-, u^+, \mathbf n)$. Two famous special cases:

  • Discontinuous Galerkin (chapter 6): $u$ and $v$ are polynomials of degree $p$ on each cell, no continuity across faces.
  • Finite volume (chapter 5): $p = 0$. With $v = 1$ the volume term vanishes ($\nabla v = 0$) and we get $|K|\,\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\bar u_K = -\int_{\partial K}\hat F\dd s$ — the integral conservation law of chapter 1, one cell at a time.

The same cell-wise idea applied to the Poisson equation gives DG methods for diffusion (chapter 8), where the numerical fluxes must handle both $u$ and $\nabla u$.

What goes wrong
  • Forgetting that test functions must vanish where $u$ is prescribed (step 4) leaves the unknown $\partial_n u$ in the equations — or, if simply dropped, imposes a wrong condition. Chapter 4 shows how Nitsche's method keeps that boundary term honestly.
  • Choosing the spaces of a mixed method independently (e.g. both continuous piecewise linear) can violate the inf–sup condition and give wildly oscillating or non-unique solutions.
