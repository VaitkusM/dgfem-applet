0Primer: fields, divergence & convergence

The handful of vector-calculus ideas the rest of the course is built on, explained with graphics analogies — and how to read a convergence plot.

A partial differential equation (PDE) relates an unknown function of several variables to its derivatives. Heat conduction, fluid flow, elasticity, electrostatics and light transport are all described by PDEs. Almost none of them can be solved with pen and paper on realistic geometries, so we discretise them: replace the unknown function by finitely many numbers and the PDE by a (large) system of algebraic equations. This primer collects the mathematical vocabulary we need for that. If you know the divergence theorem and log–log plots, skim the notation box and move on.

Notation used throughout the course

$\mathbf n$the outward unit normal on a boundary: length 1, perpendicular to the boundary, pointing out
$u(\mathbf x)$a scalar field: one number per point (temperature, density, height, …)
$\mathbf F(\mathbf x) = (F_x, F_y)$a vector field: one arrow per point (velocity, heat flux, …)

$\int_{\partial\Omega} g \dd s$line integral over the boundary, $\mathrm ds$ = arc-length element

Scalar fields, gradients and level sets

A scalar field $u:\Omega\to\R$ assigns a number to every point: think of a grey-scale image, a height map or a temperature distribution. Its gradient collects the two partial derivatives into a vector,

$$\nabla u = \begin{pmatrix} \partial_x u \\ \partial_y u \end{pmatrix}.$$

Two facts make the gradient geometric:

  • $\nabla u$ points in the direction in which $u$ increases fastest, and its length $|\nabla u|$ is that rate of increase (directional derivative: $\partial u/\partial \mathbf d = \nabla u\cdot\mathbf d$ for a unit vector $\mathbf d$).
  • $\nabla u$ is perpendicular to the level sets (contour lines) $\{u = c\}$, because along a contour $u$ does not change.
Graphics analogy

Bump mapping computes shading normals from the gradient of a height map; the contour lines below are extracted with marching squares, the 2D cousin of marching cubes. A level set $\{\phi = 0\}$ of a signed distance function is exactly how we will describe curved geometry in the chapter on immersed methods.

The Laplacian

Taking the divergence (next section) of the gradient gives the Laplacian

$$\Delta u = \nabla\cdot\nabla u = \partial_{xx} u + \partial_{yy} u .$$

It measures how much $u$ at a point differs from the average of $u$ around it. On a grid with spacing $h$ the classic approximation is

$$\Delta u(x,y) \approx \frac{u(x{+}h,y) + u(x{-}h,y) + u(x,y{+}h) + u(x,y{-}h) - 4u(x,y)}{h^2},$$

i.e. (average of the four neighbours − centre value) × $4/h^2$ — the same "umbrella operator" used for mesh smoothing. Functions with $\Delta u = 0$ are called harmonic: they equal the average of their surroundings, have no interior maxima or minima, and are as smooth as possible (try the saddle and plane fields with "colour shows Δu"). The Poisson equation $-\Delta u = f$ will be our model elliptic PDE: it describes the steady temperature in a plate heated by $f$.

Vector fields, divergence and the divergence theorem

A vector field $\mathbf F:\Omega\to\R^2$ attaches an arrow to every point, like a velocity field or a flow map texture. Its divergence is the scalar field

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf F = \partial_x F_x + \partial_y F_y .$$

Why this combination? Put a tiny square of side $h$ centred at $\mathbf x$ into the field and add up how much "stuff" the field carries out through its four sides. Through the right side (normal $(1,0)$) the outflow is about $F_x(x{+}\tfrac h2, y)\,h$; through the left side (normal $(-1,0)$) it is $-F_x(x{-}\tfrac h2,y)\,h$. Together,

$$\big(F_x(x{+}\tfrac h2,y) - F_x(x{-}\tfrac h2,y)\big)h \approx \partial_x F_x\, h^2,$$

and the top/bottom sides contribute $\partial_y F_y\,h^2$. So divergence = net outflow per unit area: positive at sources, negative at sinks, zero where whatever flows in also flows out.

Adding up the outflow of many tiny squares, all interior faces cancel (what leaves one square enters its neighbour), and only the outer boundary survives. This is the divergence theorem (Gauss' theorem), the single most important formula of this course:

$$\int_\Omega \nabla\cdot\mathbf F \dd\mathbf x \;=\; \int_{\partial\Omega} \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n \dd s .$$

Total source strength inside a region = total flux through its boundary.

The boundary integral $\int \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,\mathrm ds$ is called the flux of $\mathbf F$ through the curve; only the normal component of $\mathbf F$ counts (a flow tangential to a wall does not cross it). In 1D the theorem is just the fundamental theorem of calculus, $\int_a^b f'(x)\,\mathrm dx = f(b)\cdot(+1) + f(a)\cdot(-1)$, where $\pm1$ are the "outward normals" of the interval's two end points.

Try this
  • Vortex: the divergence is zero everywhere, so every polygon has zero net flux — even though individual edges have large fluxes.
  • Source $\mathbf F = (x,y)/2$: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F = 1$, so the net flux equals the polygon's area. Drag a vertex and watch both numbers change together.
  • Localised source: move the polygon away from the origin: the flux drops; make it enclose the origin: positive. Outside the ring $r = \sqrt 2$ the divergence is negative — the field slows down, so flux is "absorbed" there.
  • Make the polygon non-convex: the theorem still holds.

Integration by parts in several dimensions

Combine the product rule $\nabla\cdot(v\mathbf F) = v\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf F + \nabla v\cdot\mathbf F$ with the divergence theorem applied to $v\mathbf F$ and you get the multi-dimensional version of integration by parts:

$$\int_\Omega v\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf F \dd\mathbf x = \int_{\partial\Omega} v\,\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n \dd s - \int_\Omega \nabla v\cdot\mathbf F \dd\mathbf x .$$

With $\mathbf F = \nabla u$ this becomes Green's first identity (write $\partial_n u := \nabla u\cdot\mathbf n$ for the normal derivative):

$$\int_\Omega v\,\Delta u \dd\mathbf x = \int_{\partial\Omega} v\,\partial_n u \dd s - \int_\Omega \nabla v\cdot\nabla u \dd\mathbf x .$$

It moves one derivative from $u$ onto $v$ at the price of a boundary term. Every finite element, DG and FV method in this course starts with this one manoeuvre — keep an eye on the boundary term, it is where fluxes and boundary conditions enter.

Measuring errors: norms

A numerical method produces an approximation $u_h$; its error $e = u - u_h$ is again a function. To compare methods we condense it into one number, a norm:

  • max norm: $\|e\|_\infty = \max_{\mathbf x} |e(\mathbf x)|$ — the worst point;
  • $L^2$ norm: $\|e\|_{L^2(\Omega)} = \big(\int_\Omega e^2 \dd\mathbf x\big)^{1/2}$ — a root-mean-square error (the continuous cousin of the RMSE between two images);
  • $H^1$ seminorm: $|e|_{H^1} = \|\nabla e\|_{L^2}$ — the error in the gradient (for heat problems: in the heat flux).

The integrals are computed with high-order quadrature rules, never by sampling only at the mesh nodes: many methods are extra accurate at special points (“superconvergence”), and nodal sampling would make them look better than they are.

Convergence and log–log plots

Discretisations depend on a mesh of cells with size $h$. A method converges if the error goes to zero as $h\to0$; it has order $r$ if

$$\|u - u_h\| \approx C\,h^{r}\qquad (h \to 0).$$

Taking logarithms, $\log\|e\| \approx \log C + r\log h$: on a plot with logarithmic axes the error curve is a straight line of slope $r$. Halving $h$ divides the error by $2^r$: by 2 for a first-order method, by 16 for a fourth-order one. With two measurements, $r \approx \log(e_1/e_2)/\log(h_1/h_2)$. This is how every solver on this site is verified.

The simplest "discretisation" is piecewise polynomial interpolation: cut $[0,1]$ into $N$ cells of width $h = 1/N$ and on each cell replace $f$ by the polynomial of degree $p$ that matches $f$ at $p+1$ nodes. Taylor's theorem shows the error on a cell is at most about $\frac{h^{p+1}}{(p+1)!}\max|f^{(p+1)}|$, so we expect order $r = p+1$ — if $f$ is smooth (i.e. if its $(p{+}1)$-st derivative exists and is bounded).

Try this
  • Smooth function: increase $p$ and watch the slope grow as $p+1$. For $p=4$ the curve hits a floor near $10^{-15}$: round-off of double precision.
  • Steep but smooth function: for coarse meshes nothing seems to converge (“pre-asymptotic regime”), then the slope kicks in once $h$ resolves the feature.
  • Kink $|x-\tfrac13|$: no matter how large $p$ is, the $L^2$ slope stays at $1.5$ (max norm: $1$). Only the one cell containing the kink is badly approximated; its error is $O(h)$ on a set of width $h$. Smoothness, not polynomial degree, limits the rate.
  • Jump: slope $0.5$ in $L^2$ and no convergence at all in the max norm. This is why shocks in fluid flow need special treatment (chapters 5–7).
What goes wrong

Reading off a rate from the coarsest meshes (pre-asymptotic) or from the finest ones (round-off floor) gives nonsense. Always look at the whole curve, and be suspicious of a rate that is "almost" the expected one: an error in a code often shows up as an order lost (slope $p$ instead of $p+1$) rather than as an obviously wrong picture.

Summary

  • The gradient $\nabla u$ points uphill, perpendicular to level sets; the Laplacian $\Delta u = \nabla\cdot\nabla u$ compares $u$ with its local average.
  • Divergence = net outflow per unit area. Divergence theorem: $\int_\Omega\nabla\cdot\mathbf F = \int_{\partial\Omega}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n$.
  • Integration by parts moves derivatives onto a test function and produces boundary (flux) terms.
  • Errors are measured in norms ($L^2$, $H^1$, max); a method of order $r$ shows slope $r$ on a log–log plot — for smooth solutions.
