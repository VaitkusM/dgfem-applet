8DG for elliptic problems

Interior penalty methods: integrate by parts on every element, glue the pieces with Nitsche’s trick on every face, and make the glue strong enough.

In chapter 6 discontinuous Galerkin (DG) methods handled transport: a numerical flux on each face decides what crosses it, and the upwind direction makes everything stable. Diffusion is different. The diffusive flux $-\nabla u$ involves a derivative, information flows in all directions, and there is no “upwind” side to trust. This chapter builds the most popular DG method for the Poisson equation $-\Delta u = f$, the symmetric interior penalty Galerkin (SIPG) method, derives every term from scratch, and shows experimentally why its penalty parameter must be large enough — and why it must grow like $p^2/h$.

Why bother? Continuous elements already work

For the Poisson equation alone, continuous finite elements (chapter 3) are hard to beat. The reasons for a DG discretisation of the Laplacian are elsewhere:

  • Diffusion inside a DG flow solver. The Navier–Stokes equations are the Euler equations (chapter 6: DG, numerical fluxes) plus viscous terms $\nabla\cdot(\mu\nabla\mathbf u)$. The unknowns already live in a discontinuous space, so we need a DG treatment of $-\Delta$ in that same space.
  • Flexibility. Every element carries its own polynomial: the degree $p$ may change from element to element, and meshes may have hanging nodes (a big triangle next to two small ones), because nothing must match across faces.
  • Locality. The mass matrix is block diagonal (here even diagonal, see below), all couplings go through faces, data layouts are regular — ideal for GPUs (chapter 12).
  • Weak boundary conditions come for free: the same face terms that glue elements also impose $u=g$ (Nitsche, chapter 4).

But the naive idea fails completely. Take the usual weak form $\int_\Omega\nabla u\cdot\nabla v = \int_\Omega fv$ and simply allow $u_h, v_h$ to be discontinuous. Then $\int_\Omega \nabla u_h\cdot\nabla v_h = \sum_K\int_K\nabla u_h\cdot\nabla v_h$ couples nothing across faces: the matrix is block diagonal, one block per triangle, and every block is singular (a constant on $K$ has zero gradient). The elements do not talk to each other and nothing tells them the boundary values. The fix must come from face terms, and we will derive exactly which ones.

Broken spaces, jumps and averages

The DG space is the broken polynomial space

$$V_h^p = \{\, v \in L^2(\Omega) \;:\; v|_K \in \mathbb P_p(K) \text{ for every } K\in\Th \,\},$$ $$\dim V_h^p = |\Th|\cdot\frac{(p+1)(p+2)}{2},$$

where $\mathbb P_p$ are polynomials of total degree $\le p$ in $(x,y)$. Each triangle owns its own coefficients — like a “triangle soup” in graphics, where every triangle has private copies of its vertices instead of sharing them. We use the orthonormal Dubiner basis $\psi_0,\dots,\psi_{n-1}$ on the reference triangle (the same modal basis as in chapter 6): with the affine map $\mathbf x = \mathbf a + J\hat{\mathbf x}$ of each triangle, $\int_K\psi_i\psi_j = \det J\,\delta_{ij} = 2|K|\,\delta_{ij}$, so the mass matrix is diagonal.

On a face $F$ shared by $K^-$ and $K^+$, a broken function has two values (traces) $v^-$ and $v^+$. We combine them into

$$\jump{v} = v^- - v^+, \qquad \avg{w} = \tfrac12\,(w^- + w^+),$$

and we measure normal derivatives on both sides with the same normal $\n$ (out of $K^-$): $\partial_n v^\pm = \nabla v^\pm\cdot\n$. Which triangle is called “minus” is arbitrary; every face term below contains products like $\avg{\partial_n u}\,\jump{v}$ in which swapping the roles of $K^\pm$ flips both $\n$ and $\jump{v}$, so the product does not change.

An interior face $F$: $\n$ points out of $K^-$. On $F$ the jump is $\jump{u} = u^- - u^+$, the average $\avg{u} = \frac12(u^-+u^+)$.

Deriving the interior penalty method

Step 1 — integrate by parts element by element. Multiply $-\Delta u = f$ by a test function $v\in V_h^p$ and integrate over one triangle $K$. Green's formula (chapter 0) on $K$ with its outward normal $\n_K$ gives $\int_K\nabla u\cdot\nabla v - \int_{\partial K}(\nabla u\cdot\n_K)\,v = \int_K f v$. Summing over all triangles:

$$\sum_{K}\int_K \nabla u\cdot\nabla v \;-\; \sum_K\int_{\partial K} (\nabla u\cdot\n_K)\, v \;=\; \int_\Omega f v .$$

Step 2 — rewrite the element boundaries as a sum over faces. Every interior face $F$ appears twice: once from $K^-$ with $\n_{K^-} = \n$ and once from $K^+$ with $\n_{K^+} = -\n$. Together they contribute $\int_F\big(v^-\,\partial_n u^- - v^+\,\partial_n u^+\big)$. For any four numbers, $a^-b^- - a^+b^+ = (a^- - a^+)\,\tfrac{b^-+b^+}{2} + \tfrac{a^-+a^+}{2}\,(b^- - b^+)$ (multiply out to check), hence

$$\begin{aligned}\sum_K\int_{\partial K} v\;\nabla u\cdot\n_K \;=\; &\sum_{F\in\mathcal F_h^{i}}\int_F\Big(\jump{v}\,\avg{\partial_n u} + \avg{v}\,\jump{\partial_n u}\Big)\\ &+\; \sum_{F\in\mathcal F_h^{\partial}}\int_F v\;\partial_n u .\end{aligned}$$

With the boundary conventions $\jump{v} = v$, $\avg{w} = w$ on $\mathcal F_h^\partial$, the last sum is just the first term over boundary faces.

Step 3 — use what we know about the exact solution. If $u$ is smooth enough ($u\in H^2$), then $u$ and its normal flux are continuous across every interior face: $\jump{u} = 0$ and $\jump{\partial_n u} = 0$. The $\avg{v}\jump{\partial_n u}$ term disappears and the exact solution satisfies

$$\sum_K\int_K\nabla u\cdot\nabla v \;-\; \sum_{F\in\mathcal F_h}\int_F \avg{\partial_n u}\,\jump{v} \;=\; \int_\Omega f v \qquad\text{for all } v\in V_h^p. \tag{$*$}$$

This is already a candidate method — but its matrix is not symmetric and, worse, not stable. Two more terms are added, both chosen so that the exact solution still satisfies the equation (consistency):

Step 4 — symmetrise. Add $-\theta\sum_F\int_F\avg{\partial_n v}\,\jump{u}$. For the exact solution $\jump{u} = 0$ on interior faces, and $\jump{u} = u = g$ on boundary faces, so the same quantity $-\theta\sum_{F\in\mathcal F_h^\partial}\int_F \partial_n v\, g$ goes to the right-hand side. With $\theta = 1$ the bilinear form becomes symmetric in $u$ and $v$.

Step 5 — penalise jumps. Add $\sum_F\sigma_F\int_F\jump{u}\jump{v}$, again zero on interior faces for the exact solution and equal to $\sum_{F\in\mathcal F_h^\partial}\sigma_F\int_F g\,v$ on the boundary. This term is the “glue”: it punishes $u_h$ for jumping.

Interior penalty DG (IPDG)

Find $u_h\in V_h^p$ such that $a_h(u_h, v) = \ell_h(v)$ for all $v\in V_h^p$, where

$$\begin{aligned}a_h(u,v) = &\sum_K\int_K\nabla u\cdot\nabla v \;-\; \sum_{F\in\mathcal F_h}\int_F\Big(\avg{\partial_n u}\jump{v} + \theta\,\avg{\partial_n v}\jump{u}\Big)\\ &+\; \sum_{F\in\mathcal F_h}\sigma_F\int_F\jump{u}\jump{v},\end{aligned}$$ $$\ell_h(v) = \int_\Omega f v \;+\; \sum_{F\in\mathcal F_h^\partial}\int_F\big(\sigma_F\, v - \theta\,\partial_n v\big)\,g .$$

$\theta = 1$: SIPG (symmetric), $\theta = -1$: NIPG (non-symmetric), $\theta = 0$: IIPG (incomplete). Our penalty is $$\sigma_F = C_{IP}\,\frac{(p+1)(p+2)}{2}\,\frac{|F|}{\min(|K^-|,|K^+|)} = C_{IP}\,\frac{(p+1)(p+2)}{h_F},$$ with $h_F = 2\min(|K^-|,|K^+|)/|F|$ ($h_F$ is the smaller height of the two triangles over $F$; on boundary faces only $K^-$ counts).

Because the exact solution satisfies $a_h(u, v) = \ell_h(v)$, subtracting the discrete equation gives Galerkin orthogonality $a_h(u - u_h, v) = 0$ for all $v\in V_h^p$. A quick consequence (and a good unit test): if the exact solution is itself a polynomial of degree $\le p$, it lies in $V_h^p$ and IPDG reproduces it exactly, up to round-off — for all three variants.

Penalty = Nitsche’s method on every face

Look at a boundary face. The face terms read $-\int_F\partial_n u\, v - \theta\int_F\partial_n v\,(u - g) + \sigma_F\int_F (u-g)\,v$ — exactly Nitsche’s method for the Dirichlet condition $u = g$ from chapter 4: the consistency term, the symmetry term and the penalty $\gamma/h$. On an interior face the same three terms appear with $u - g$ replaced by $\jump{u} = u^- - u^+$: each triangle treats the neighbour's trace as “Dirichlet data” for itself, weakly. SIPG is Nitsche's method applied on every face of the mesh, and the boundary condition is just the special case where the neighbour is the data $g$.

The difference to a pure penalty method (only $\sigma_F\int\jump{u}\jump{v}$) is the same as in chapter 4: without the consistency term $\avg{\partial_n u}\jump{v}$ the exact solution does not satisfy the discrete equations, and one must let $\sigma_F\to\infty$ to converge, which ruins the conditioning.

Graphics analogy

Welding a triangle soup. Every triangle has private vertex copies; the penalty is a spring between coincident copies (a soft constraint, as in a physics engine). Springs alone either let the mesh “crack” (too soft) or make the system stiff (too hard). Nitsche's extra terms are what a physicist would call the consistent constraint force: the exact, continuous solution feels no spring force at all, so the springs only need to be stiff enough to keep the discrete system stable — not infinitely stiff.

Try this
  • Lower $C_{IP}$ below about $0.3$ with SIPG: $\lambda_{\min}$ turns negative. Go further and vary $N$: the error becomes erratic. With $p = 1$, $N = 12$, $C_{IP} = 0.05$ the $L^2$ error exceeds $10$ (the matrix is nearly singular), while neighbouring meshes may look harmless — an indefinite discretisation gives no guarantee at all.
  • Raise $C_{IP}$ to $100$: the jumps shrink (the colour of the edges fades) and $u_h$ approaches the continuous FE solution, but the matrix gets stiffer.
  • Switch to NIPG and lower $C_{IP}$: $\lambda_{\min}$ of the symmetric part stays positive for every $C_{IP} > 0$. The section below explains why.
  • Try the Gaussian peak with $p = 1$ vs $p = 3$ on the same mesh.

SIPG, NIPG and IIPG

The three variants differ only in the sign of the symmetrisation term. Put $u = v$ in $a_h$:

$$a_h(v,v) = \sum_K\|\nabla v\|_K^2 \;-\; (1+\theta)\sum_F\int_F\avg{\partial_n v}\jump{v} \;+\; \sum_F\sigma_F\|\jump{v}\|_F^2 .$$
  • NIPG ($\theta = -1$): the middle term vanishes, $a_h(v,v) = \sum_K\|\nabla v\|^2_K + \sum_F\sigma_F\|\jump v\|^2_F$, which is positive for every $v\ne 0$ as soon as all $\sigma_F > 0$ (if it is zero, $v$ is constant on each triangle, continuous across faces and zero on $\partial\Omega$, so $v=0$). NIPG is coercive for any positive penalty.
  • SIPG ($\theta = 1$): the middle term has the factor $2$ and no sign — it must be dominated by the penalty. In exchange the matrix is symmetric, so it is SPD when the penalty is large enough and conjugate gradients (chapter 3) apply.
  • IIPG ($\theta = 0$): factor $1$, non-symmetric; simplest to implement.

All three converge in the DG energy norm $|||v|||^2 = \sum_K\|\nabla v\|_K^2 + \sum_F\sigma_F\|\jump v\|_F^2$ with the optimal rate $h^p$. In $L^2$ only SIPG is optimal, $h^{p+1}$, for every $p$: its symmetry makes the method adjoint consistent, so the duality argument (Aubin–Nitsche, chapter 3) gains one extra power of $h$. NIPG and IIPG are not adjoint consistent; in our experiments they still reach $h^{p+1}$ for odd $p$ ($p = 1, 3$) but only $h^p$ for $p = 2$ — the classical even-degree suboptimality. With a large penalty the jumps are tiny and the loss only becomes visible on fine meshes.

Why the penalty must be large (and scale like p²/h)

For SIPG, $a_h(v,v)$ contains the indefinite term $-2\sum_F\int_F\avg{\partial_n v}\jump{v}$. With Cauchy–Schwarz and Young's inequality $2ab \le \delta a^2 + b^2/\delta$ (for any $\delta > 0$),

$$2\Big|\int_F\avg{\partial_n v}\jump{v}\Big| \;\le\; \delta_F\,\|\avg{\partial_n v}\|_F^2 + \frac{1}{\delta_F}\|\jump v\|_F^2 .$$

The first term must be paid for by $\sum_K\|\nabla v\|_K^2$, the second by the penalty. So the question is: how large can the normal derivative of a polynomial be on an edge, compared with its size inside the element? That is a trace inequality.

The 1D answer is explicit. Let $L_j = \sqrt{(2j+1)/2}\,P_j$ be the orthonormal Legendre polynomials on $[-1,1]$, with $P_j(1) = 1$. A polynomial $w = \sum_{j\le p} c_j L_j$ has $\|w\|^2 = \sum_j c_j^2$ and $w(1) = \sum_j c_j\sqrt{(2j+1)/2}$, so by Cauchy–Schwarz

$$w(1)^2 \le \Big(\sum_{j=0}^p c_j^2\Big)\Big(\sum_{j=0}^p\frac{2j+1}{2}\Big) = \frac{(p+1)^2}{2}\,\|w\|^2_{L^2(-1,1)},$$

with equality for $c_j\propto\sqrt{(2j+1)/2}$. On an interval of length $h$ this becomes $w(\text{end})^2 \le \frac{(p+1)^2}{h}\|w\|^2$: a polynomial of degree $p$ can pile up near an endpoint, and the larger $p$ the more it can. On triangles the sharp analogue (Warburton & Hesthaven) is

$$\|w\|_F^2 \;\le\; \frac{(p+1)(p+2)}{2}\,\frac{|F|}{|K|}\,\|w\|_K^2 \qquad (w\in\mathbb P_p(K)),$$

which is exactly the factor in our $\sigma_F$. Applying it to $\nabla v\in\mathbb P_{p-1}$ and choosing $\delta_F\sim h_F/p^2$ gives

$$a_h(v,v) \;\ge\; \tfrac12\sum_K\|\nabla v\|_K^2 + \sum_F\Big(\sigma_F - c_\star\frac{p^2}{h_F}\Big)\|\jump v\|_F^2,$$

with a constant $c_\star$ that depends only on the shape of the triangles. SIPG is coercive once $\sigma_F$ beats $c_\star p^2/h_F$ — the penalty must scale like $p^2/h$. With our normalisation, the critical constant $C_{IP}^\star$ (where $\lambda_{\min}$ changes sign) is about $0.33$–$0.6$ for $p = 1,2,3$ and essentially independent of the mesh size; the widget measures it.

What goes wrong
  • Penalty too small (SIPG/IIPG): the matrix is indefinite or even singular, CG breaks down, and the solution can be garbage. Because the threshold depends on $p$ and on the element shapes (thin triangles have small $h_F$), a fixed $\sigma_F = 10/h_F$ that is safe for $p = 1, 2$ is no longer coercive for $p = 3$ (see the table in the widget above: without the $p$-factor the critical constant grows like $p(p+1)$).
  • Penalty too large: still convergent (the method is consistent), but the largest eigenvalues grow proportionally to $\sigma_F$, so the condition number and the number of CG iterations grow, and the jumps are suppressed so strongly that DG loses its flexibility.
  • Sign conventions: mixing “$\n$ out of $K^-$” with “$\jump{v} = v^+ - v^-$” flips the sign of the consistency term; the exact solution then no longer satisfies the discrete equations and the method is wrong, often without any visible warning on coarse meshes. Fix the conventions once (here: AGENTS.md) and test with a polynomial solution, which a consistent method must reproduce to round-off.

The price of discontinuity: unknowns and non-zeros

DG pays for its flexibility with more unknowns: every triangle stores $(p+1)(p+2)/2$ coefficients, while continuous elements share vertex and edge nodes. On an $N\times N$ grid split into $2N^2$ triangles, CG of degree $p$ with strong Dirichlet conditions has $(pN-1)^2$ unknowns, SIPG has $N^2(p+1)(p+2)$. For $p = 1$ that is $6N^2/(N-1)^2$, i.e. six to eight times more on moderate meshes; for large $N$ the ratio approaches $(p+1)(p+2)/p^2$, which decreases towards $1$ as $p$ grows. Each DG row couples one triangle to itself and its three neighbours, so the number of matrix non-zeros grows like $4\cdot\big((p+1)(p+2)/2\big)^2$ per triangle.

A word on LDG

Interior penalty is not the only option. The local discontinuous Galerkin (LDG) method (Cockburn & Shu) introduces the flux $\boldsymbol\sigma = -\nabla u$ as a second unknown, writes $-\Delta u = f$ as the first-order system $\boldsymbol\sigma + \nabla u = 0$, $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma = f$, and discretises both equations with DG and numerical fluxes, exactly like chapter 6. Because $\boldsymbol\sigma$ can be eliminated element by element, LDG also ends up as a system for $u$ only. Arnold, Brezzi, Cockburn and Marini showed that IP, LDG and many other DG methods for diffusion are all instances of one framework, distinguished by their choice of numerical fluxes. Chapter 9 takes this first-order viewpoint further: mixed methods, hybridization and HDG.

Summary

  • A broken polynomial space alone does not discretise $-\Delta u$: the elements decouple. Face terms are needed.
  • Element-wise integration by parts + the identity $\sum_K\int_{\partial K}v\,\partial_{n_K}u = \sum_F\int_F(\jump v\avg{\partial_n u} + \avg v\jump{\partial_n u})$ gives the consistency term $-\int_F\avg{\partial_n u}\jump v$.
  • Adding $-\theta\int_F\avg{\partial_n v}\jump u$ and $\sigma_F\int_F\jump u\jump v$ keeps consistency; it is Nitsche's method on every face. Boundary conditions are the special case “neighbour = data $g$”.
  • SIPG ($\theta=1$): symmetric, optimal in $L^2$, needs $\sigma_F \gtrsim p^2/h_F$. NIPG ($\theta=-1$): coercive for any $\sigma_F>0$, but loses an order in $L^2$ for even $p$. IIPG ($\theta = 0$) behaves like NIPG in $L^2$.
  • The $p^2/h$ scaling comes from polynomial trace inequalities: polynomials can concentrate near edges.
  • DG has more unknowns and non-zeros than CG — the motivation for hybridization in chapter 9.
