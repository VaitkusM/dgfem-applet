1Fluxes & conservation laws

What a flux is physically, how “conservation” becomes a PDE, and why nonlinear transport develops shocks.

Most PDEs of physics are balance laws: the amount of something (mass, heat, momentum) inside a region changes only because it flows across the region's boundary or is produced inside. The quantity describing the flow is the flux. Getting fluxes right — physically and numerically — is the common thread of every method in this course.

What is a flux?

A flux is a vector field $\mathbf F$ whose normal component $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n$ tells how much of a quantity crosses a small piece of curve per unit length and per unit time. The total amount crossing a curve $\Gamma$ per unit time is $\int_\Gamma \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n \dd s$. Flux vectors come from physics — they are the constitutive part of a model:

  • Advective flux $\mathbf F = \mathbf a\,u$: stuff of density $u$ carried by a velocity $\mathbf a$ (smoke in wind, dye in water). In gas dynamics the mass flux is $\rho\mathbf u$.
  • Diffusive flux $\mathbf F = -k\nabla u$ (Fourier's law for heat, Fick's law for concentrations): stuff flows down the gradient, from hot to cold, at a rate proportional to the steepness.
  • Nonlinear fluxes $\mathbf F(u)$: in traffic flow the car flux is density × speed, and the speed depends on the density; in gas dynamics the momentum flux is $\rho \mathbf u\otimes\mathbf u + p\mathbf I$.
Graphics analogy

Radiometry! Irradiance is the radiant flux through a surface per unit area. Light arriving in direction $\boldsymbol\omega$ deposits energy proportional to $\boldsymbol\omega\cdot\mathbf n = \cos\theta$ — Lambert's cosine law is exactly the "only the normal component of the flux counts" rule.

The conservation law

Pick any fixed region $V$. Let $M(t) = \int_V u \dd\mathbf x$ be the amount inside. Without sources, it can only change through the boundary:

$$\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int_V u \dd\mathbf x \;=\; -\int_{\partial V} \mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n \dd s \qquad\text{for every region } V.$$

(The minus sign: $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n > 0$ means outflow, which decreases $M$.) This integral form is the physical law.

Apply the divergence theorem to the right-hand side and move the time derivative inside the integral: $\int_V \big(\partial_t u + \nabla\cdot\mathbf F\big) \dd\mathbf x = 0$. Since this holds for every region $V$, the integrand itself must vanish (if it were positive somewhere, a tiny $V$ around that point would give a positive integral). We get the differential form

$$\partial_t u + \nabla\cdot\mathbf F(u) = 0,$$

a conservation law. With a source term $s$ (heating, chemical reactions) the right-hand side becomes $s$. Keep in mind the logical order: the integral form is the fundamental one; the differential form needs $u$ to be differentiable, and we will soon meet solutions that are not.

Model problems: transport, diffusion, Poisson

Inserting the two basic fluxes into the conservation law gives the advection–diffusion equation

$$\partial_t u + \nabla\cdot(\mathbf a u) - \nabla\cdot(k\nabla u) = s,\qquad \mathbf F = \underbrace{\mathbf a u}_{\text{advective}} \underbrace{-\,k\nabla u}_{\text{diffusive}} .$$

Its two parts behave very differently:

  • Advection ($k=0$) is hyperbolic: information travels with finite speed along curves (characteristics, below); profiles are transported, sharp fronts stay sharp.
  • Diffusion is parabolic: it smooths everything instantly, with infinite propagation speed.
  • When nothing changes in time ($\partial_t u = 0$), $\mathbf a = 0$ and $k = 1$, we get the Poisson equation $-\Delta u = s$, which is elliptic: an equilibrium in which every point influences every other point. It needs boundary data all around $\partial\Omega$.

Characteristics: how transport carries information

Take linear advection in 1D, $u_t + a u_x = 0$ with constant $a$, and follow an observer moving with speed $a$: $x(t) = x_0 + a t$. By the chain rule, the value they see changes at the rate

$$\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt} u(x(t), t) = u_t + \frac{\mathrm dx}{\mathrm dt} u_x = u_t + a u_x = 0 .$$

So $u$ is constant along the straight lines $x - at = x_0$, the characteristics, and the solution is $u(x,t) = u_0(x - at)$: the initial profile slides to the right without changing shape. A boundary condition is needed only where characteristics enter the domain (the inflow boundary) — a fact we will use for boundary conditions and upwinding.

Nonlinear transport: Burgers' equation and shocks

The simplest nonlinear conservation law is the (inviscid) Burgers equation

$$u_t + \Big(\frac{u^2}{2}\Big)_x = 0 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad u_t + u\,u_x = 0 \quad\text{(for smooth } u\text{)}.$$

It is advection with a speed equal to the solution itself: large values move faster. The same argument as above shows that $u$ is constant along characteristics $x = x_0 + u_0(x_0)\,t$ — straight lines, but now with different slopes. Where $u_0$ decreases, fast fluid behind catches up with slow fluid ahead, and the characteristics eventually cross. The first crossing happens at the breaking time

$$t^* = -\frac{1}{\min_x u_0'(x)} .$$

After $t^*$ the method of characteristics would give several values at the same point (the dashed "overturning wave" below), which is physically meaningless. What really happens: a discontinuity, a shock, forms and moves on.

The Rankine–Hugoniot condition

How fast does a shock move? Use the integral form on an interval $[x_a, x_b]$ containing a discontinuity at position $x_s(t)$, with state $u_L$ to its left and $u_R$ to its right:

$$\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\Big[u_L\,(x_s - x_a) + u_R\,(x_b - x_s)\Big] = f(u_L) - f(u_R) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad s\,(u_L - u_R) = f(u_L) - f(u_R),$$

where $s = \mathrm dx_s/\mathrm dt$. This is the Rankine–Hugoniot condition $s = [f]/[u]$: the shock speed is the ratio of the flux jump to the state jump. For Burgers, $s = \frac{u_L^2/2 - u_R^2/2}{u_L - u_R} = \frac{u_L + u_R}{2}$. Not every discontinuity satisfying it is physical: characteristics must run into the shock from both sides ($f'(u_L) > s > f'(u_R)$, the Lax entropy condition). If instead $u_L < u_R$, the jump immediately opens into a continuous rarefaction fan.

The Riemann problem

The initial-value problem with a single jump, $u_0(x) = u_L$ for $x < 0$ and $u_R$ for $x > 0$, is called a Riemann problem. Its solution depends only on $x/t$ (it is self-similar), and for Burgers it is either a shock with speed $(u_L+u_R)/2$ or a fan $u = x/t$. Riemann problems are the building blocks of finite volume and DG methods: every cell face where two different values meet is a little Riemann problem.

Systems: the Euler equations of gas dynamics

Fluid flow conserves several quantities at once: mass, momentum and energy. In 1D, with density $\rho$, velocity $u$, pressure $p$ and total energy density $E$, the Euler equations read

$$\partial_t \begin{pmatrix}\rho\\ \rho u\\ E\end{pmatrix} + \partial_x \begin{pmatrix}\rho u\\ \rho u^2 + p\\ u\,(E+p)\end{pmatrix} = 0, \qquad p = (\gamma - 1)\Big(E - \tfrac12\rho u^2\Big),$$

with $\gamma = 1.4$ for air. It is still $\partial_t \mathbf U + \partial_x \mathbf F(\mathbf U) = 0$, just with vectors. Information now travels along three families of characteristics with speeds $u - c$, $u$ and $u + c$, where $c = \sqrt{\gamma p/\rho}$ is the speed of sound; their Riemann problems contain shocks, rarefactions and contact discontinuities. Chapter 5 solves the famous Sod shock tube.

Preview: numerical fluxes

A computer stores $u$ by finitely many numbers per cell. On a cell face the two neighbouring cells generally disagree: the left cell says $u^-$, the right cell says $u^+$. Which flux crosses the face? We need a rule, a numerical flux $\hat F(u^-, u^+)$, with three properties:

  1. Consistency: $\hat F(u, u) = f(u)$ — if both sides agree, use the physical flux.
  2. Conservation: what leaves one cell through the face enters the other one, exactly. Then the discrete total mass is conserved, just like the integral law.
  3. Stability / upwinding: information must be taken from where it comes from (the direction of the characteristics).

The "exact" choice is to solve the little Riemann problem at the face and take the flux of its solution at $x/t = 0$ — Godunov's idea. Chapter 5 develops this into the finite volume method; chapter 6 into discontinuous Galerkin.

What goes wrong

Writing Burgers in the non-conservative form $u_t + u u_x = 0$ and discretising that gives shocks moving at the wrong speed — the two forms are only equivalent for smooth solutions. Numerical methods for conservation laws must be built on the conservative (flux) form, so that they converge to the solution of the integral law, including the correct Rankine–Hugoniot speed.

Summary

  • A flux $\mathbf F$ measures transport across curves; only $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n$ counts.
  • Integral conservation law $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int_V u = -\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n$ $\Rightarrow$ differential form $u_t + \nabla\cdot\mathbf F = 0$ for smooth $u$.
  • Advective fluxes transport along characteristics (hyperbolic); diffusive fluxes $-k\nabla u$ smooth (parabolic); the steady diffusion problem is the Poisson equation (elliptic).
  • Nonlinear fluxes make characteristics cross: shocks form, moving with the Rankine–Hugoniot speed $s = [f]/[u]$.
  • Numerical methods need numerical fluxes $\hat F(u^-,u^+)$: consistent, conservative, upwind.
