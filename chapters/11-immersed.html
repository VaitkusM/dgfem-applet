11Immersed boundary methods

Solve on a simple background grid that ignores the geometry: cut cells and CutFEM, WEB-splines, the Shifted Boundary Method — and why tiny cut cells are the enemy.

Every method so far needed a mesh that fits the domain: element edges lie on the boundary. Immersed (or unfitted, embedded, fictitious-domain) methods drop this requirement. The domain is given implicitly — typically by a level set — and is simply laid over a Cartesian background grid. The boundary cuts through cells wherever it likes. The price is paid in three places: integration over cut cells, boundary conditions on a boundary that is not made of element edges, and stability when a cell is cut very unluckily. This chapter builds three complete answers for the Poisson problem $-\Delta u = f$ in $\Omega$, $u = g$ on $\Gamma = \partial\Omega$ and verifies all of them.

Why immersed methods?

In industrial simulation, generating a good boundary-fitted mesh is frequently the most time-consuming step, often needing more human time than solving the PDE. Reasons:

  • CAD geometry is dirty. Trimmed NURBS patches with gaps, overlaps and tiny features must be "healed" before a mesher accepts them.
  • Geometry moves. A beating heart valve, a falling droplet, a rotating propeller, shape optimisation: a fitted mesh must deform and be re-meshed again and again; a background grid stays put.
  • Geometry is often implicit already. Medical CT/MRI data and 3D scans are voxel images; level-set methods for free surfaces and topology optimisation describe shapes by a function $\phi$; procedural modelling builds shapes from signed distance functions.
  • Cartesian grids are fast. Structured data, trivial neighbour lookup, perfect for GPUs (chapter 12), multigrid and octrees.

Immersed methods turn the meshing problem into a geometric query problem: "which side of $\Gamma$ is this point on?", "where does $\Gamma$ cross this edge?", "which point of $\Gamma$ is closest?". Those are exactly the queries computer graphics answers every day.

Geometry as a function: level sets and SDFs

A level-set function $\phi:\R^2\to\R$ describes a domain by its sign: $\Omega = \{\mathbf x:\phi(\mathbf x) < 0\}$, $\Gamma = \{\phi = 0\}$. Where $\nabla\phi\ne 0$ on $\Gamma$, the outward normal is $\mathbf n = \nabla\phi/|\nabla\phi|$ (the gradient points towards increasing $\phi$, i.e. outwards). The best-behaved level set is the signed distance function (SDF): $|\phi(\mathbf x)| = \operatorname{dist}(\mathbf x,\Gamma)$, so that $|\nabla\phi| = 1$ almost everywhere. We use two shapes, both centred at $\mathbf c$ and expressed in polar coordinates $(r,\theta)$ around $\mathbf c$:

  • Circle: $\phi(\mathbf x) = |\mathbf x - \mathbf c| - R$. This is an exact SDF; the closest point on $\Gamma$ is $\mathbf c + R\,(\mathbf x - \mathbf c)/|\mathbf x - \mathbf c|$.
  • Flower: boundary $r = R(\theta) := R\,(1 + a\cos(k(\theta - \rho)))$ with $0\le a<1$, and $\phi = r - R(\theta)$. This $\phi$ has the correct sign (negative inside, zero exactly on $\Gamma$), but it is not a distance: along a ray from the centre it measures the radial gap, and near the petals the radial gap is larger than the true distance. (It is also not differentiable at $r = 0$, which is harmless because that point is deep inside.) Its area is $\tfrac12\int_0^{2\pi} R(\theta)^2\dd\theta = \pi R^2(1 + a^2/2)$.

Closest points. The Shifted Boundary Method needs, for a point $\mathbf p$, the closest point on $\Gamma$. With the parametrisation $\mathbf X(t) = \mathbf c + R(t)(\cos t,\sin t)$ we minimise $D(t) = \tfrac12|\mathbf X(t) - \mathbf p|^2$: solve $D'(t) = (\mathbf X - \mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf X' = 0$ by Newton's method, $t \leftarrow t - D'(t)/D''(t)$ with $D'' = |\mathbf X'|^2 + (\mathbf X - \mathbf p)\cdot\mathbf X''$. Because the petals create several local minima, we start Newton from every local minimum of $D$ sampled at $16k$ angles and keep the best result. The tests check that the result lies on $\Gamma$ to machine precision, that $\mathbf p - \mathbf X(t)$ is perpendicular to the tangent, and that no point of a fine polyline of $\Gamma$ is closer.

Graphics analogy

Sphere tracing (Hart 1996) renders an SDF by marching along a ray with step size $|\phi(\mathbf x)|$: the SDF guarantees that a ball of that radius contains no surface. It would fail with our flower $\phi$, which over-estimates the distance near the petals — the same reason the cut quadrature below needs a Lipschitz bound to decide that a triangle is certainly not cut. Extracting $\Gamma$ from samples of $\phi$ on a grid is marching squares (2D marching cubes, Lorensen & Cline 1987); on triangles it is "marching triangles", which is exactly the linear cut below.

Background mesh, active mesh and cut cells

Lay a uniform triangle mesh $\mathcal T_h$ over a box containing $\Omega$. Evaluate $\phi$ at the vertices. An element whose three vertex values are negative is inside, all non-negative is outside, and mixed signs make it a cut cell. Inside and cut elements form the active mesh; unknowns live on its vertices — including vertices outside $\Omega$!

Cut quadrature by the linear cut. On each element replace $\phi$ by its linear interpolant $\phi_h$ (exactly what a rasteriser does with a vertex attribute). The zero set of a linear function is a straight segment. If $\phi$ changes sign along the edge from vertex $A$ to vertex $B$, the segment crosses that edge at

$$P = A + t\,(B - A),\qquad t = \frac{\phi_A}{\phi_A - \phi_B}\in[0,1].$$

With one vertex inside, $K\cap\Omega_h$ is a small triangle; with two vertices inside, it is a quadrilateral, which we split into two triangles. Any standard triangle quadrature on these sub-triangles integrates polynomials exactly over $K\cap\Omega_h$, and a Gauss rule on the segment integrates over $\Gamma_h\cap K$ with the constant normal $\mathbf n_h = \nabla\phi_h/|\nabla\phi_h|$.

Geometric error. $\Gamma_h$ is the polygon through points on (or near) $\Gamma$; a chord of length $h$ deviates from an arc of curvature $\kappa$ by about $\kappa h^2/8$. Hence $|\Omega_h| - |\Omega| = O(h^2)$ and $|\Gamma_h| - |\Gamma| = O(h^2)$ — tested for both shapes. Two cures when more is needed:

  • Recursive refinement: split each possibly cut triangle into 4 children (edge midpoints, true $\phi$ evaluated there), repeat $L$ times, then cut linearly. The error drops by about $4^{-L}$ at the cost of more quadrature points; the background mesh and the unknowns do not change. A child is declared uncut without further splitting when all its vertex values have the same sign and $\min|\phi| > \text{Lip}(\phi)\cdot\operatorname{diam}$.
  • Curved cut: find the exact edge crossings (1D root finding on each edge) and integrate the inside part as "curved triangles" $\{A + r\,(\mathbf C(\tau) - A)\}$ whose curved side $\mathbf C(\tau)$ follows the parametrised $\Gamma$. This removes the geometric error up to quadrature error (the tests reproduce area and perimeter to $10^{-12}$); the WEB-spline solver below needs it to show its higher-order rates.

The small-cut problem

Look at a vertex outside $\Omega$ whose neighbouring elements are all cut, each with a tiny inside part. Its hat function $\varphi_i$ is a perfectly normal function on the background mesh, but it is integrated only over $\operatorname{supp}\varphi_i\cap\Omega$. If that region has area $\varepsilon$, the diagonal entry $A_{ii} = \int_{\Omega}|\nabla\varphi_i|^2$ is $O(\varepsilon/h^2)$ instead of $O(1)$: the system has a (nearly) zero eigenvalue, and the condition number $\kappa(A)$ is unbounded — not because of $h$, but because of where $\Gamma$ happens to cut. This is the small-cut problem. It has two faces:

  • Elliptic problems: ill-conditioning (iterative solvers stall, direct solvers lose digits), and also loss of stability of Nitsche's method, because its penalty argument (chapter 4) needs the full element, not the sliver inside $\Omega$ — see below.
  • Explicit time stepping for transport (finite volumes, DG): a cut cell with volume $\alpha_K|K|$ but faces of length $\sim h$ obeys a CFL condition of roughly $\Delta t \lesssim \alpha_K h/|\mathbf a|$. A single sliver cell with $\alpha_K = 10^{-6}$ would force a million times more time steps.

Every immersed method is, at its heart, a strategy against small cuts: penalise them (CutFEM), merge them with neighbours (agglomeration, AgFEM), extrapolate them away (WEB-splines), redistribute their updates (state redistribution) or avoid them altogether (SBM).

CutFEM: Nitsche on $\Gamma$ plus ghost penalty

CutFEM (Hansbo & Hansbo 2002; Burman & Hansbo 2012; Burman et al. 2015) uses continuous P1 elements on the active mesh. Since $\Gamma$ is not made of element edges, the Dirichlet condition cannot be built into the space; it is imposed weakly with Nitsche's method exactly as in chapter 4, only now the boundary integrals run over the segments $\Gamma_h\cap K$ inside the cut elements. Find $u_h$ in the P1 space of the active mesh such that for all test functions $v$

$$\begin{aligned} &\underbrace{\int_{\Omega_h}\nabla u_h\cdot\nabla v - \int_{\Gamma_h}\partial_n u_h\,v - \int_{\Gamma_h}\partial_n v\,u_h + \frac{\gamma}{h}\int_{\Gamma_h}u_h v}_{\text{Nitsche (ch. 4) on the cut boundary}} \;+\; \underbrace{g_h(u_h, v)}_{\text{ghost penalty}}\\ &\qquad= \int_{\Omega_h} f v - \int_{\Gamma_h}\partial_n v\,g + \frac{\gamma}{h}\int_{\Gamma_h} g\,v . \end{aligned}$$

All volume integrals use the sub-triangles, all boundary integrals the cut segments. $h$ is the background cell width, never the size of the cut part. Here $u$ is defined everywhere, so we evaluate $g = u$ on $\Gamma_h$ and in effect solve on $\Omega_h$; the $O(h^2)$ geometric error of $\Omega_h$ is of the same order as the P1 $L^2$ error, so it does not spoil the rates.

Why Nitsche alone is not enough. Testing with $v = u_h$ gives $\|\nabla v\|^2_{\Omega_h} - 2\int_{\Gamma_h}\partial_n v\,v + \frac\gamma h\|v\|^2_{\Gamma_h}$. In chapter 4 the middle term was controlled with the inverse trace inequality $h\|\partial_n v\|^2_{\Gamma\cap K}\le C_I\|\nabla v\|^2_{K}$. On a cut element this inequality still holds with the whole element $K$ on the right (for P1, $\nabla v$ is constant and $|\Gamma\cap K|\le\operatorname{diam}K$), but the bilinear form only controls $\|\nabla v\|^2_{K\cap\Omega}$, which can be arbitrarily smaller. On a sliver, no fixed $\gamma$ wins, and indeed the tests find negative eigenvalues of the CutFEM matrix without stabilisation for an ordinary circle on a $16\times16$ grid with $\gamma = 10$.

The ghost penalty (Burman 2010) adds, on the set $\mathcal F_G$ of interior faces of the active mesh that belong to at least one cut element,

$$g_h(u, v) = \gamma_g\sum_{F\in\mathcal F_G} h\int_F [\partial_n u]\,[\partial_n v]\dd s ,\qquad [\partial_n v] = (\nabla v|_{K^-} - \nabla v|_{K^+})\cdot\mathbf n_F .$$

For a continuous P1 function the tangential derivative along a face is continuous, so the jump of the gradient across $F$ is purely normal: $\nabla v|_{K^-} - \nabla v|_{K^+} = [\partial_n v]\,\mathbf n_F$. Therefore $|\nabla v|_{K^-}|^2 \le 2|\nabla v|_{K^+}|^2 + 2[\partial_n v]^2$, and multiplying by $|K^-|\sim h^2$:

$$\|\nabla v\|^2_{K^-}\;\le\; C\Big(\|\nabla v\|^2_{K^+} + h\int_F[\partial_n v]^2\dd s\Big).$$

Walking face by face from an inside element to each cut element gives the key estimate $\|\nabla v\|^2_{\text{active mesh}}\le C\big(\|\nabla v\|^2_{\Omega_h} + g_h(v,v)\big)$ with $C$ independent of how $\Gamma$ cuts the mesh. Now the inverse trace inequality on the whole element is affordable, Nitsche is stable again for a fixed $\gamma$, and the condition number obeys $\kappa(A)\le C h^{-2}$ as for a fitted mesh. The penalty is consistent: the exact solution is smooth, so its normal derivative does not jump and $g_h(u,v) = 0$ — the test that CutFEM reproduces a linear solution exactly checks this. (For P$p$ elements one penalises jumps of all normal derivatives up to order $p$, with weights $h^{2j-1}$.)

Intuition

The ghost penalty says: "on cut elements, don't let the solution bend more than it would if the neighbouring element's linear function were simply continued". It is a smoothness prior on the fictitious part of the active mesh — the only part where the PDE itself gives no information.

The Shifted Boundary Method

The Shifted Boundary Method (SBM, Main & Scovazzi 2018) takes a radical shortcut: no cut cells at all.

  1. Surrogate domain. $\tilde\Omega_h$ = union of background elements lying inside $\Omega$. We take every triangle whose three vertices satisfy $\phi < 0$ (for the convex circle this is exactly "$K\subset\Omega$"; in the concave parts of the flower $\Gamma$ may bulge slightly into such a triangle). Other selections exist in the literature; the method only needs $\tilde\Gamma_h$ to be $O(h)$-close to $\Gamma$.
  2. Surrogate boundary $\tilde\Gamma_h = \partial\tilde\Omega_h$, a staircase of mesh edges, with outward normal $\tilde{\mathbf n}$.
  3. Distance vector. For $\tilde{\mathbf x}\in\tilde\Gamma_h$, $\mathbf d(\tilde{\mathbf x}) = M(\tilde{\mathbf x}) - \tilde{\mathbf x}$ points to the closest point $M(\tilde{\mathbf x})$ on $\Gamma$ (computed with the closest-point routine above).
  4. Shift the boundary condition by Taylor. We want $u(M(\tilde{\mathbf x})) = g(M(\tilde{\mathbf x}))$, but we only have unknowns on $\tilde\Omega_h$. Expand: $$u(\tilde{\mathbf x} + \mathbf d) = u(\tilde{\mathbf x}) + \nabla u(\tilde{\mathbf x})\cdot\mathbf d + O(|\mathbf d|^2),$$ so on $\tilde\Gamma_h$ we impose the shifted condition $u + \nabla u\cdot\mathbf d = \bar g$, with $\bar g(\tilde{\mathbf x}) := g(M(\tilde{\mathbf x}))$. Since $|\mathbf d| = O(h)$, the neglected term is $O(h^2)$ — consistent with second-order P1 accuracy.

The condition is imposed à la Nitsche on $\tilde\Gamma_h$. The weak form of Main & Scovazzi (Part I, Poisson) reads: find $u_h$ on $\tilde\Omega_h$ such that for all $v$

$$\int_{\tilde\Omega_h}\nabla v\cdot\nabla u_h - \int_{\tilde\Gamma_h} v\,\nabla u_h\cdot\tilde{\mathbf n} - \int_{\tilde\Gamma_h}\nabla v\cdot\tilde{\mathbf n}\,\big(u_h + \nabla u_h\cdot\mathbf d\big) + \frac{\alpha}{h}\int_{\tilde\Gamma_h}\big(v + \nabla v\cdot\mathbf d\big)\big(u_h + \nabla u_h\cdot\mathbf d\big)$$ $$= \int_{\tilde\Omega_h} v f - \int_{\tilde\Gamma_h}\nabla v\cdot\tilde{\mathbf n}\;\bar g + \frac{\alpha}{h}\int_{\tilde\Gamma_h}\big(v + \nabla v\cdot\mathbf d\big)\,\bar g .$$

Read it term by term: the first two terms are integration by parts on $\tilde\Omega_h$ (consistency); the third is Nitsche's symmetrising term, applied to the shifted residual $u_h + \nabla u_h\cdot\mathbf d - \bar g$; the last is the penalty on that residual, tested with the shifted test function. For $\mathbf d = 0$ it is exactly the symmetric Nitsche method of chapter 4 on $\tilde\Omega_h$. With $\mathbf d\ne0$ the matrix is not symmetric ($\nabla u\cdot\mathbf d$ appears in the third term, but $\nabla v\cdot\mathbf d$ does not appear in the second). For P1 elements $\nabla u_h$ is constant per element, so each boundary term only involves the one surrogate triangle adjacent to the boundary edge.

What we gain: only full elements — no cut quadrature, no small cuts, standard assembly; the geometry enters only through $\mathbf d$. What we pay: the solution lives on $\tilde\Omega_h$ (errors are measured there; extending it to the strip between $\tilde\Gamma_h$ and $\Gamma$ again uses the Taylor expansion), a non-symmetric matrix, and the need for closest points. For comparison we also implement the unshifted variant: $\mathbf d = 0$ in the operator but the data still transferred, $\bar g(\tilde{\mathbf x}) = g(M(\tilde{\mathbf x}))$ — i.e. pretending the staircase is the true boundary. That commits an $O(h)$ error in the boundary condition and the $L^2$ rate drops to 1, while the shifted SBM is second order (tested for both shapes). The shifted SBM is exact for linear solutions, because then $u + \nabla u\cdot\mathbf d = u(\tilde{\mathbf x} + \mathbf d)$ holds exactly.

WEB-splines: weighted extended B-splines

WEB-splines (Höllig, Reif & Wipper 2001) attack both problems — boundary conditions and small cuts — with two ideas, built on uniform tensor-product B-splines.

B-splines. The cardinal B-spline $N_n$ of degree $n$ is the box function $\chi_{[0,1)}$ convolved with itself $n$ times: a piecewise polynomial of degree $n$, $C^{n-1}$ smooth, non-negative, supported on $[0, n+1]$, and its integer shifts sum to one. (The cubic one is the "B-spline filter" of image resampling and the basis of uniform cubic spline curves.) On a grid of width $h$ with index coordinates $\xi = (x - x_0)/h$, $\eta = (y-y_0)/h$, $$b_k(x,y) = N_n(\xi - k_1)\,N_n(\eta - k_2),$$ supported on the $(n+1)\times(n+1)$ grid cells with lower-left indices $k_1\ldots k_1+n$, $k_2\ldots k_2+n$. Unlike P1 hats, spline spaces are smooth and need far fewer unknowns per accuracy, but nothing about them fits a curved boundary.

Idea 1 — weight functions make the Dirichlet condition strong. Choose a smooth weight function $w$ with $w > 0$ in $\Omega$, $w = 0$ on $\Gamma$, and $|\nabla w|\ne 0$ on $\Gamma$ (linear growth, like a distance). Every function $w\,b_k$ vanishes on $\Gamma$ exactly, no matter how the grid cuts it — the homogeneous Dirichlet condition is built into the space, as in chapter 3, without a fitted mesh. We use

  • circle: $w = (R^2 - |\mathbf x - \mathbf c|^2)/(2R)$, a polynomial with $w\approx R - r$ near $\Gamma$;
  • flower: Höllig's regularised distance $w = \tfrac{\delta}{3}\big(1 - (1 - t)^3\big)$ with $t = -\phi/\delta$ for $0\le t\le 1$ and $w = \delta/3$ deeper inside. It is $C^2$, $w\approx-\phi$ near $\Gamma$, and with $\delta = 0.4\min_\theta R(\theta)$ the non-smooth centre of $\phi$ lies where $w$ is constant.

(R-functions of Rvachev give smooth weights for domains built by Boolean operations — the CSG trees of solid modelling.)

Idea 2 — extension makes the basis stable. Classify the grid cells (inside, cut, outside) and the B-splines:

  • $b_k$ is relevant if its support meets $\Omega$;
  • inner if its support contains at least one grid cell lying entirely inside $\Omega$;
  • outer if it is relevant but not inner — its support overlaps $\Omega$ only near $\Gamma$, possibly in a tiny sliver where $w$ is also tiny.

Outer B-splines are exactly the small-cut troublemakers. Instead of dropping them (which would destroy the approximation order near $\Gamma$), WEB-splines extrapolate their coefficients from inner ones. The reason this works is Marsden's identity: for uniform knots, $(\xi - \tau)^n = \sum_k \psi_k(\tau)\,N_n(\xi - k)$ with $\psi_k(\tau) = \prod_{\mu=1}^{n}(k + \mu - \tau)$, and more generally the B-spline coefficients of any polynomial of degree $\le n$ are polynomials of degree $\le n$ in the index $k$. So the coefficient of an outer B-spline can be computed from inner coefficients by polynomial extrapolation in index space without losing polynomial reproduction.

For every outer index $j$ pick the $(n+1)\times(n+1)$ array of inner indices $I(j) = \ell + \{0,\ldots,n\}^2$ closest to $j$ (we take the array whose centre $\ell + (n/2, n/2)$ is nearest to $j$ in the Euclidean distance of index space). The extension coefficients are the values at $j$ of the tensor-product Lagrange polynomials on these nodes:

$$e_{i,j} = \prod_{\nu=1}^{2}\;\prod_{\substack{\mu=0\\ \mu\ne i_\nu - \ell_\nu}}^{n}\frac{j_\nu - \ell_\nu - \mu}{i_\nu - \ell_\nu - \mu},\qquad i\in I(j),$$

so that $\sum_{i\in I(j)} e_{i,j}\,q(i) = q(j)$ for every polynomial $q$ of coordinate degree $\le n$. The WEB-spline basis consists of one function per inner index $i$,

$$B_i = \frac{w}{w(\mathbf x_i)}\Big(b_i + \sum_{j\in J(i)} e_{i,j}\, b_j\Big),\qquad J(i) = \{\text{outer } j : i\in I(j)\},$$

where $\mathbf x_i$ is the centre of an inside grid cell in the support of $b_i$ (we take the one with largest $w$; the normalisation only affects conditioning). The extended B-splines $b_i + \sum_j e_{i,j}b_j$ reproduce every polynomial of coordinate degree $\le n$ on $\Omega$ (tested with Marsden's coefficients), so the WEB space approximates like the full spline space — Höllig, Reif & Wipper prove optimal order and a condition number $O(h^{-2})$ independent of how $\Gamma$ cuts the grid.

Non-homogeneous data. Take any smooth extension $\tilde g$ of $g$ into $\Omega$ ($\tilde g = g$ on $\Gamma$) and seek $u_h = \tilde g + \sum_i c_i B_i$; since every $B_i$ vanishes on $\Gamma$, $u_h = g$ on $\Gamma$ exactly (tested). The Galerkin equations are, for every inner $i$,

$$\sum_k c_k\int_\Omega\nabla B_k\cdot\nabla B_i = \int_\Omega f\,B_i - \int_\Omega\nabla\tilde g\cdot\nabla B_i .$$

Caveat: if $\tilde g$ happened to be the exact solution, the correction would vanish and nothing would be tested. We therefore use $\tilde g = (1 - w)\,G$, where $G = \cos(\pi x)e^y$ is the formula of the boundary data (here it coincides with $u$); then the unknown part $(u - \tilde g)/w = G$ is non-trivial. Integrals are computed cell by cell with Gauss rules on inside cells and the curved cut quadrature on cut cells.

Variants and relatives

  • Finite cell method (FCM; Parvizian, Düster & Rank 2007): high-order ($p$-version) elements on the background mesh; the fictitious part is kept but given a tiny material coefficient (e.g. $10^{-8}$ times the physical one) instead of being cut away, and cut cells are integrated by adaptive (quadtree) subdivision — the recursive refinement above. Dirichlet conditions are typically imposed weakly (penalty or Nitsche).
  • Aggregated FEM (AgFEM; Badia, Verdugo & Martín 2018): each badly cut cell is aggregated with a nearby interior "root" cell, and the degrees of freedom that live only on badly cut cells are eliminated by extending the polynomial of the root cell — a discrete extension operator very much in the spirit of WEB-spline extension, yielding condition numbers independent of the cut.
  • Cell agglomeration in cut DG: discontinuous Galerkin (chapters 6 and 8) allows arbitrary element shapes, so a small cut cell can simply be merged with a neighbour into one bigger element with a single polynomial (Johansson & Larson 2013 for elliptic problems; Müller, Krämer-Eis, Kummer & Oberlack 2017 for compressible flow with immersed boundaries).
  • Cut-cell finite volumes fight the time-step restriction: the h-box method (Berger, Helzel & LeVeque 2003) computes fluxes from boxes of regular length $h$ that reach into neighbouring cells, and state redistribution (Berger & Giuliani 2021) performs the unstable update with the regular time step and then redistributes the solution conservatively over overlapping neighbourhoods of small cells.
  • SBM extensions: Part II of Main & Scovazzi covers advection–diffusion and Navier–Stokes; Atallah, Canuto & Scovazzi analysed the method and extended it to Neumann conditions (the "second-generation" SBM) and to higher order (higher-order Taylor expansions, which need higher derivatives of $u_h$).
  • The original "immersed boundary method" (Peskin 1972, 2002) is a different animal, designed for fluid–structure interaction (blood flow through heart valves). The fluid lives on a Cartesian grid; the elastic structure is a set of Lagrangian marker points. The structure's elastic forces are spread to the grid with a regularised (smoothed) delta function a few cells wide, and the markers move with the fluid velocity interpolated back with the same kernel. The boundary is thus smeared over a few cells, with typically only first-order accuracy near the interface. Immersed finite element methods like CutFEM and the SBM are instead "sharp-interface" methods: the boundary enters the weak form exactly (or through a controlled Taylor shift). The name is shared; the mathematics is not.
What goes wrong
  • Ignoring small cuts. A cut-cell code without ghost penalty, aggregation or extension works on most geometries and then fails on one unlucky position — the widget above shows how sharp those spikes are. Never judge a cut-cell method on a single configuration.
  • Using $|K\cap\Omega|$ as "h" in the Nitsche penalty sounds natural but makes the penalty explode on slivers and the system even worse conditioned; use the background element size.
  • Quadrature that is too crude. The geometric error of the integration domain caps the convergence rate: the linear cut gives at most second order — enough for P1, not for the higher-degree WEB-splines, which here need the curved cut.
  • Level sets that are not distances. Tests like "$|\phi| > \operatorname{diam}K$ ⇒ not cut" or sphere tracing assume an SDF (or a known Lipschitz bound). The flower $\phi = r - R(\theta)$ is not one.
  • Forgetting the shift. Imposing the boundary data on the staircase $\tilde\Gamma_h$ as if it were $\Gamma$ costs one order of accuracy.

Summary

  • Immersed methods replace boundary-fitted meshing by geometric queries on a level set $\phi$: sign, edge crossings, closest points.
  • Cut quadrature: linear interpolation of $\phi$ gives straight cut segments and sub-triangles with $O(h^2)$ geometric error; refinement or curved cuts reduce it.
  • The small-cut problem — arbitrarily small volume fractions $\alpha_K$ — destroys conditioning (and explicit time steps) unless the method is stabilised.
  • CutFEM = Nitsche on $\Gamma_h$ + ghost penalty $\gamma_g\sum_F h\int_F[\partial_n u][\partial_n v]$: optimal P1 rates and $\kappa(A) = O(h^{-2})$ for every cut.
  • SBM: no cut cells; impose $u + \nabla u\cdot\mathbf d = g(\tilde{\mathbf x} + \mathbf d)$ on the surrogate boundary. The shift restores second order.
  • WEB-splines: weight function $w$ for strong Dirichlet conditions, extension of outer B-splines for stability; rate $n+1$ in $L^2$ for degree $n$.
