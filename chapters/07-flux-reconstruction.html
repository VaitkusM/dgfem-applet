7Flux reconstruction

Huynh's differential view: build a continuous flux polynomial per element, differentiate it at the solution points — and recover DG without a single integral.

Chapter 6 derived DG from integrals: weak forms, mass matrices, quadrature. In 2007 H. T. Huynh showed that the same scheme (and a whole family of relatives) can be written as a pure collocation method: evaluate the flux at a few points, correct it with a fixed polynomial so that it becomes continuous across elements, and differentiate. This flux reconstruction (FR) formulation is simple to code, cheap to run, and has a tunable parameter that trades accuracy for larger time steps. It is the basis of production solvers such as PyFR.

The differential view

We still solve $u_t + f(u)_x = 0$ with a polynomial of degree $p$ per element, stored by its values at $p+1$ solution points. Instead of a weak form, FR asks that the PDE hold exactly at the solution points, with $f(u)$ replaced by a cleverly built flux polynomial. In reference coordinates ($\partial_x = \frac2h\partial_\xi$) the recipe for one evaluation of $\partial_t u$ is:

  1. Discontinuous flux. In every element, compute $f(u_{k,i})$ at the solution points and let $F^D_k(\xi) = \sum_i f(u_{k,i})\,\ell_i(\xi)$ be its interpolant. Neighbouring $F^D_k$ do not match at the faces: the flux, like $u_h$, jumps.
  2. Common interface fluxes. Extrapolate $u_h$ to the faces and evaluate a numerical flux $\hat F(u^-, u^+)$ — exactly as in FV and DG (chapters 5, 6).
  3. Correct. Add a multiple of the left correction function to fix the left end and a multiple of the right one to fix the right end: $$F_k(\xi) = F^D_k(\xi) + \big(\hat F_{k-\frac12} - F^D_k(-1)\big)\, g_L(\xi) + \big(\hat F_{k+\frac12} - F^D_k(1)\big)\, g_R(\xi).$$ Because $g_L(-1) = 1$, $g_L(1) = 0$ and $g_R(-1) = 0$, $g_R(1) = 1$, we get $F_k(-1) = \hat F_{k-\frac12}$ and $F_k(1) = \hat F_{k+\frac12}$: neighbouring elements now agree on the face value, and the global flux is continuous.
  4. Differentiate at the solution points: $$\begin{aligned}\frac{\mathrm d u_{k,i}}{\mathrm dt} &= -\frac2h\, F_k'(\xi_i)\\ &= -\frac2h\Big[(D\,\mathbf f_k)_i + \big(\hat F_{k-\frac12} - F^D_k(-1)\big)\,g_L'(\xi_i) + \big(\hat F_{k+\frac12} - F^D_k(1)\big)\,g_R'(\xi_i)\Big],\end{aligned}$$ where $\mathbf f_k = \big(f(u_{k,0}),\dots,f(u_{k,p})\big)$, so that $(D\,\mathbf f_k)_i = (F^D_k)'(\xi_i)$.

No integrals, no mass matrix: only point evaluations, a differentiation matrix and two precomputed vectors $g_L'(\xi_i)$, $g_R'(\xi_i)$. Since $F_k$ has degree $p+1$, its derivative has degree $p$, the same as $u_h$; the two degree-$p$ polynomials $\partial_t u_h$ and $-\frac2h F_k'$ agree at $p+1$ points, so they are equal everywhere on the element. Integrating over the element therefore gives $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int_{K_k} u_h = -\big(F_k(1) - F_k(-1)\big) = -\big(\hat F_{k+\frac12} - \hat F_{k-\frac12}\big)$: FR is conservative, just like DG.

Graphics analogy

Think of $F^D$ as separately fitted curve pieces that do not meet at the joints. Correction functions are like blending ramps that pull each piece's endpoint to an agreed common value — the left ramp is $1$ at the left joint and $0$ at the right one — similar to how a $C^0$ spline is stitched together from independent segments. The shape of the ramp in between is the design freedom of FR.

Correction functions

A left correction function is any polynomial $g_L$ of degree $p+1$ with $g_L(-1) = 1$ and $g_L(1) = 0$; the right one is its mirror image $g_R(\xi) = g_L(-\xi)$. That leaves $p$ degrees of freedom, and their choice determines the scheme. All classical choices are built from the right Radau polynomial

$$R_{R,k}(\xi) = \frac{(-1)^k}{2}\big(P_k(\xi) - P_{k-1}(\xi)\big),\qquad R_{R,k}(-1) = 1,\quad R_{R,k}(1) = 0,$$

(using $P_k(1) = 1$ and $P_k(-1) = (-1)^k$). Huynh's three most important choices are

$$g_{DG} = R_{R,p+1},\qquad g_{2} = \frac{p\,R_{R,p+1} + (p+1)\,R_{R,p}}{2p+1},$$ $$g_{Ga} = \frac{(p+1)\,R_{R,p+1} + p\,R_{R,p}}{2p+1} = \frac{(-1)^p}{2}(1-\xi)\,P_p(\xi).$$
  • $g_{DG}$ reproduces DG (next section).
  • $g_2$ allows a markedly larger time step than $g_{DG}$, and with GLL solution points it reproduces the DG spectral element method of chapter 6 (GLL collocation, lumped mass) — our tests confirm this to round-off, for advection and for Burgers.
  • $g_{Ga}$ vanishes at the $p$ Gauss points (the zeros of $P_p$) and at $\xi = 1$; the last expression follows from $\xi P_p = \frac{(p+1)P_{p+1} + pP_{p-1}}{2p+1}$.

Why $g_{DG}$ gives DG

Take the nodal DG method in strong form (integrate by parts twice) with the exact mass matrix and the flux interpolated at the solution points, $f_h = F^D$. For every test polynomial $v$ of degree $\le p$:

$$\frac h2\int_{-1}^1 \partial_t u_h\, v\dd\xi = -\int_{-1}^1 (F^D)'\, v\dd\xi + \big(\hat F_{k-\frac12} - F^D(-1)\big)\,v(-1) - \big(\hat F_{k+\frac12} - F^D(1)\big)\,v(1).$$

Now multiply the FR update $\partial_t u_h = -\frac2h F'$ by $\frac h2 v$ and integrate:

$$\frac h2\int \partial_t u_h\, v = -\int (F^D)' v - \big(\hat F_{k-\frac12} - F^D(-1)\big)\int g_L' v - \big(\hat F_{k+\frac12} - F^D(1)\big)\int g_R' v .$$

The two agree for all $v$ exactly when $\int g_L' v = -v(-1)$ and $\int g_R' v = v(1)$ for every $v$ of degree $\le p$. For $g_L = R_{R,p+1}$, integrate by parts: $\int g_L' v = [g_L v]_{-1}^{1} - \int g_L v' = -v(-1) - \int g_L v'$, and the last integral vanishes because $g_L \propto P_{p+1} - P_p$ is orthogonal to all polynomials of degree $\le p-1$, in particular to $v'$. The mirror identity for $g_R$ follows by substituting $\xi \to -\xi$. Hence:

FR with $g_{DG}$ is identical to nodal DG in strong form with exact mass matrix and collocated flux — for any flux, any solution points. For a linear flux the collocated flux is exact, so FR($g_{DG}$) is then simply the DG method of chapter 6 (weak or strong form, exact integration).

For a nonlinear flux the equivalent DG scheme interpolates $f(u_h)$ instead of integrating it exactly, i.e. it is aliased; over-integrated DG is a different scheme (our tests show the difference for Burgers). In the same way one checks that FR with $g_2$ on GLL points is the DG spectral element method.

The VCJH family: one parameter, many schemes

Vincent, Castonguay and Jameson (2011) found a one-parameter family of correction functions for which the scheme is provably stable for linear advection:

$$g_L = \frac{(-1)^p}{2}\Big[P_p - \frac{\eta_p\,P_{p-1} + P_{p+1}}{1+\eta_p}\Big],\qquad \eta_p = \frac{c\,(2p+1)\,(a_p\,p!)^2}{2},\qquad a_p = \frac{(2p)!}{2^p\,(p!)^2}.$$

One checks $g_L(-1) = 1$ and $g_L(1) = 0$ directly from $P_n(\pm1) = (\pm1)^n$. Special values of $c \ge 0$:

parameter$\eta_p$correction function
$c = 0$$0$$g_{DG}$ (DG)
$c_{SD} = \dfrac{2p}{(2p+1)(p+1)(a_p\,p!)^2}$$\dfrac{p}{p+1}$$g_{Ga}$ (a spectral difference scheme, Jameson 2010)
$c_{HU} = \dfrac{2(p+1)}{(2p+1)\,p\,(a_p\,p!)^2}$$\dfrac{p+1}{p}$$g_2$ (Huynh)
$c \to \infty$$\infty$$R_{R,p}$ (degree only $p$)

(The identities in the table are verified in our tests by comparing Legendre coefficients.) For $c \ge 0$ the schemes are energy stable for linear advection with an upwind flux — not in the plain $L^2$ norm but in a Sobolev-type norm that adds, on each element, $\tfrac c2$ times the squared $p$-th derivative $(\partial_\xi^p u_h)^2$ (in reference coordinates). Consistently, all eigenvalues of the semi-discrete operator have $\mathrm{Re}\,\lambda \le 0$ in our tests. VCJH schemes can also be read as DG with a modified mass matrix, or as a filtered DG method (Allaneau & Jameson 2011).

Time step versus accuracy

Increasing $c$ changes the spectrum so that the largest stable CFL number grows, from $c = 0$ (DG) through $c_{SD}$ and $c_{HU}$ up to a maximum at some $c_+ > c_{HU}$ (computed numerically by the widget below; it depends on the RK method), after which it decreases again. The price is accuracy: for $0 \le c \le 64\,c_{HU}$ the error grows monotonically with $c$ (tested for $p = 1,\dots,4$). In our tests, $c = 64\,c_{HU}$ is more than ten times less accurate than DG for $p = 3$ and $N = 16$, while $c = c_{HU}/2$ and $2c_{HU}$ still converge at the full rate $p+1$. (For very large $c$ the behaviour on practical meshes becomes irregular; explore it in the accuracy widget.)

What goes wrong
  • Comparing the wrong DG. "FR with $g_{DG}$ equals DG" is only true for the matching variant (collocated flux, exact mass). Comparing against over-integrated DG for a nonlinear flux will show differences of the size of the discretisation error.
  • Aliasing. FR is a collocation method, so nonlinear fluxes are always aliased (like DG-SEM). Under-resolved simulations may need filtering or anti-aliasing.
  • Shocks. FR oscillates at discontinuities exactly like DG; it needs limiting or artificial viscosity.
  • Too large $c$. Bigger time steps are tempting, but beyond $c_+$ the CFL limit shrinks again, and the accuracy is worse than with DG.

For a linear flux the FR update does not even depend on where the solution points are: the flux interpolant is exact, so the evolving polynomial is the same for Gauss and for GLL points (our tests compare the two to round-off). The choice of points matters only for nonlinear fluxes (aliasing) and for cost.

Summary

  • FR evaluates the flux at solution points, corrects the discontinuous flux polynomial with $g_L$, $g_R$ so that it takes common values $\hat F$ at the faces, and differentiates.
  • Correction functions: degree $p+1$, $g_L(-1) = 1$, $g_L(1) = 0$, $g_R(\xi) = g_L(-\xi)$; built from Radau polynomials.
  • $g_{DG} = R_{R,p+1}$ recovers nodal DG (strong form, collocated flux) exactly; $g_2$ on GLL points recovers DG-SEM.
  • The VCJH family ($c \ge 0$) is linearly stable; larger $c$ gives larger time steps up to $c_+$, at the cost of accuracy.
  • FR is conservative, simple and cheap — and as oscillatory at shocks as DG.
