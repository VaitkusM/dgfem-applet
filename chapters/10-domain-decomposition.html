10Domain decomposition

Divide and conquer for PDE solvers: overlapping Schwarz methods, coarse spaces, Schur complements and interface problems.

A fine mesh in 2D or 3D produces millions of unknowns. No single processor can, or should, solve such a system on its own. Domain decomposition cuts the domain into pieces, solves small problems on the pieces (in parallel, with fast direct solvers) and makes the pieces agree through an iteration. Two ideas carry the whole field. Subdomains must exchange information through their boundaries, either by overlapping (Schwarz methods) or through an interface problem (Schur complement methods). And because a local exchange moves information only to the neighbours, every scalable method needs a small global coarse problem.

Why divide the domain?

Three reasons, from hardware to mathematics:

  • Parallel computers have distributed memory. A cluster is many computers connected by a network; each one can only touch its own memory quickly. The natural way to distribute a mesh is to give each processor a contiguous piece of the domain (a subdomain). Work on the piece is local; communication happens only across the piece's boundary, whose size (a curve in 2D) is much smaller than the piece itself (an area). This surface-to-volume effect is why domain decomposition scales.
  • Direct solvers do not scale. The banded LU of chapter 3 (reverse Cuthill–McKee + band elimination) applied to our $N\times N$ grid problem has bandwidth $w = N - 1 = \sqrt n$ (checked in the tests), so its cost $\approx n w^2$ grows like $n^2$, and its memory like $n w = n^{3/2}$. Cut the square into $K\times K$ boxes and each box costs $(n/K^2)^2$: all of them together cost $n^2/K^2$, a factor $K^2$ less, and they can run at the same time. The price: the pieces must be glued together by an iteration.
  • Iterative solvers alone are slow. Plain conjugate gradients needs about $\sqrt{\kappa(A)}$ iterations, and for the Laplacian $\kappa(A) \sim h^{-2}$ (for our matrix exactly $\kappa(A) = \cot^2(\pi h/2) \approx 4/(\pi h)^2$, see below). Every refinement of the mesh doubles the iteration count. Domain decomposition provides preconditioners that remove this growth.
Graphics analogy: tiles and halos

A GPU renders the screen in tiles, and a parallel image filter splits a picture into blocks, one per core. A blur of radius $r$ cannot be computed tile by tile: each tile must first read a halo (ghost cells, a guard band) of $r$ pixels owned by its neighbours. Overlapping Schwarz methods are exactly this: each subdomain reads an overlap region from its neighbours, solves, and writes back (only) its own part. And just as a very wide blur needs either many halo exchanges or a coarse image pyramid (mipmaps), a PDE solver needs either many iterations or a coarse grid problem to spread information across the whole domain.

Schwarz's alternating method (1870)

Domain decomposition is older than computers. In 1870 Hermann Amandus Schwarz wanted to prove that the Laplace equation $\Delta u = 0$ with given boundary values has a solution on complicated domains. For a disc the solution is known explicitly (Poisson's integral formula) and for a rectangle too (Fourier series). For the union of a disc and a rectangle (figure) Schwarz proposed to alternate between the two simple shapes:

Alternating Schwarz method. Start with a guess $u_2^0$. For $n = 1, 2, \dots$

$$\begin{aligned} &\text{solve on } \Omega_1:& -\Delta u_1^n &= f \text{ in } \Omega_1, & u_1^n &= g \text{ on } \partial\Omega_1\cap\partial\Omega, & u_1^n &= u_2^{n-1} \text{ on } \Gamma_1,\\ &\text{solve on } \Omega_2:& -\Delta u_2^n &= f \text{ in } \Omega_2, & u_2^n &= g \text{ on } \partial\Omega_2\cap\partial\Omega, & u_2^n &= u_1^{n} \text{ on } \Gamma_2. \end{aligned}$$

Each subproblem takes its missing boundary values on the artificial boundary from the latest solution of the other subdomain.

Why does it converge? Look at the simplest case: $-u'' = f$ on $(0,1)$ with $\Omega_1 = (0,\beta)$ and $\Omega_2 = (\alpha,1)$, $\alpha<\beta$. The errors $e_i^n = u - u_i^n$ satisfy the same iteration with $f = 0$ and zero boundary values at $x=0$ and $x=1$, so they solve $-e'' = 0$: they are straight lines. Let $E = e_2^{n-1}(\beta)$ be the error that $u_1^n$ receives at $x = \beta$.

  1. $e_1^n$ is the line through $(0, 0)$ and $(\beta, E)$, so at $x = \alpha$ it equals $e_1^n(\alpha) = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}E$.
  2. $e_2^n$ is the line through $(\alpha, \frac{\alpha}{\beta}E)$ and $(1, 0)$, so at $x = \beta$ it equals $e_2^n(\beta) = \frac{\alpha}{\beta}\cdot\frac{1-\beta}{1-\alpha}\,E$.

Every full iteration multiplies the error by

$$\rho = \frac{\alpha\,(1-\beta)}{\beta\,(1-\alpha)} \;<\; 1 .$$

The convergence factor depends only on the geometry. With a wide overlap ($\alpha$ small, $\beta$ large) $\rho$ is small. As the overlap shrinks ($\beta\to\alpha$), $\rho\to 1$: without overlap the method stalls, because the two subproblems then only pass each other's boundary value back and forth. In 2D and 3D the errors are harmonic functions instead of lines, and the maximum principle gives the same qualitative result: a geometric contraction that gets worse as the overlap gets thinner.

Overlapping Schwarz methods in matrix form

On the computer we work with the discrete system $A\mathbf u = \mathbf b$. Cut the unit square into $K_x\times K_y$ boxes $\Omega_k$, then grow every box by $\ell$ layers of cells into $\Omega_k'$. Neighbouring subdomains now overlap in strips of width $\delta = 2\ell h$. The unknowns strictly inside $\Omega_k'$ form the local problem. Let $R_k$ be the 0/1 matrix that picks them out of a global vector.

The local correction. Suppose $\mathbf u^n$ is the current iterate and $\mathbf r = \mathbf b - A\mathbf u^n$ its residual. The exact error $\mathbf e = \mathbf u - \mathbf u^n$ solves $A\mathbf e = \mathbf r$. We cannot afford that, so we look for the best correction that lives only in subdomain $k$: $\mathbf e \approx R_k^T\mathbf v$, a vector that is zero outside $\Omega_k'$. We ask that the residual equations hold at the unknowns of subdomain $k$: $R_k A R_k^T \mathbf v = R_k\mathbf r$. Hence

$$\mathbf u^{n+1} = \mathbf u^n + R_k^T A_k^{-1} R_k(\mathbf b - A\mathbf u^n), \qquad A_k = R_k A R_k^T .$$

$A_k$ consists of the rows and columns of $A$ that belong to $\Omega_k'$. It is precisely the finite element matrix of the Poisson problem on $\Omega_k'$ with Dirichlet conditions on $\partial\Omega_k'$, and the vertices on $\partial\Omega_k'$ keep their current values. So this one line is Schwarz's subdomain solve: "solve on $\Omega_k'$ with boundary data taken from the current global iterate". (In the energy inner product, $P_k = R_k^T A_k^{-1}R_kA$ is the orthogonal projection onto the functions supported in $\Omega_k'$. The local correction is the best one possible in that subspace.)

There are two ways to combine the $K$ local corrections:

Multiplicative Schwarz (sequential, like Gauss–Seidel): for $k = 1, \dots, K$ in turn, $\;\mathbf u \leftarrow \mathbf u + R_k^T A_k^{-1}R_k(\mathbf b - A\mathbf u)$. Each subdomain uses the newest values of the subdomains before it. With $K = 2$ this is Schwarz's alternating method.

Additive Schwarz (simultaneous, like Jacobi): all subdomains use the same residual and their corrections are added,

$$M_{\rm ASM}^{-1} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} R_k^T A_k^{-1} R_k, \qquad \mathbf u \leftarrow \mathbf u + M_{\rm ASM}^{-1}(\mathbf b - A\mathbf u).$$

The analogy is exact, not only a metaphor. If every "subdomain" is a single unknown, without overlap, then $R_k = \mathbf e_k^T$, $A_k = a_{kk}$, and $M_{\rm ASM}^{-1} = \mathrm{diag}(A)^{-1}$ is the Jacobi method, while the multiplicative sweep is Gauss–Seidel. Schwarz methods are block versions of these, with large, overlapping blocks.

The additive version is the parallel one: all $A_k^{-1}$ are applied at the same time, and only the overlap values must be exchanged. But used as a plain iteration it usually diverges. Unknowns in an overlap receive corrections from two (or four) subdomains, and the sum overshoots. The tests check this for $2\times2$ subdomains. Its real use is as a preconditioner for a Krylov method (next section).

Restricted additive Schwarz (RAS) (Cai & Sarkis, 1999) avoids the double counting. Every subdomain solves on its overlapping region but writes back only the values it owns:

$$M_{\rm RAS}^{-1} = \sum_{k=1}^{K} R_k^T D_k A_k^{-1} R_k, \qquad \textstyle\sum_k R_k^T D_k R_k = I .$$

This is the halo pattern of the analogy box: read the halo, write only your tile. It also saves communication, because no overlap values are sent back. The price: $M_{\rm RAS}^{-1}$ is not symmetric, so it is combined with GMRES instead of CG.

Schwarz methods as preconditioners

Recall that CG converges in about $\sqrt{\kappa}$ iterations, with $\kappa$ the ratio of the largest to smallest eigenvalue. Preconditioned CG runs the same algorithm on $M^{-1}A$. It needs $M^{-1}$ to be symmetric positive definite, which $M_{\rm ASM}^{-1}$ is (each term $R_k^TA_k^{-1}R_k$ is symmetric positive semi-definite, and together they cover every unknown; checked numerically in the tests). One application of $M_{\rm ASM}^{-1}$ is one round of independent local solves, i.e. one parallel step. How many such steps are needed? The classical estimates (Dryja & Widlund; see Toselli & Widlund) for the Poisson problem with subdomains of diameter $H$ and overlap $\delta$ are

$$\begin{aligned} \kappa\big(M_{\rm ASM}^{-1}A\big) &\;\le\; C\Big(1 + \frac{1}{H\delta}\Big) &&\text{(one-level)},\\ \kappa\big(M_{\rm ASM,2}^{-1}A\big) &\;\le\; C\Big(1 + \frac{H}{\delta}\Big) &&\text{(two-level, with a coarse space)}, \end{aligned}$$

with $C$ independent of $h$, $H$ and $\delta$ (the domain has diameter 1). Read them carefully:

  • Both bounds are independent of the mesh size $h$ when $\delta$ is a fixed fraction of $H$ ("generous overlap"). That is the first success: the $h^{-2}$ growth of $\kappa(A)$ has disappeared.
  • The one-level bound grows when the subdomains get smaller. With $\delta \sim H$ it is $\sim 1/H^2$, i.e. proportional to the number of subdomains $K$ in 2D. Using more processors makes each iteration cheaper but requires more iterations. The method is not scalable.
  • With a coarse space the bound depends only on the ratio $H/\delta$. Keep it fixed and the number of iterations stays bounded however many subdomains are used.

Why one-level methods cannot scale

Consider the corner source of the widget above and a one-level additive method. One application of $M^{-1}$ updates subdomain $k$ using only the residual inside $\Omega_k'$. Information therefore moves by about one subdomain per iteration. After $n$ iterations, subdomains more than $n$ boxes away (counting diagonal neighbours) have not "heard" of the source at all: the iterate there is exactly zero. The tests check this for a point load with $5\times5$ subdomains, for ASM+CG and RAS+GMRES. But the solution of an elliptic problem is non-zero everywhere: every point feels every source (chapter 1). So at least about $K_x - 1$ iterations are necessary, and the count must grow with the number of subdomains. Note that the cause is not a slow local solver; even exact local solves cannot beat this bound.

The coarse space

The fix is a small global problem. Put a coarse grid with nodes at the subdomain corners and use its bilinear (Q1) hat functions, interpolated to the fine grid, as the columns of $R_0^T$. The two-level preconditioner adds the Galerkin coarse correction

$$M_{\rm ASM,2}^{-1} = R_0^T A_0^{-1} R_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{K} R_k^T A_k^{-1} R_k, \qquad A_0 = R_0 A R_0^T .$$

$A_0$ is tiny: one unknown per interior coarse node, $(K_x-1)(K_y-1)$ in total. It couples all subdomains directly, so a source anywhere influences the correction everywhere after one iteration (also verified in the tests). Physically, the coarse problem carries the smooth, long-range part of the solution that local solves cannot see, and the local solves fix the rough part. This is the same division of labour as in multigrid. The same coarse term can be added to RAS.

What goes wrong
  • Measuring speed by iteration counts only. One-level methods with few subdomains look fine. The trouble appears at hundreds or thousands of subdomains, exactly where parallel computers live. Always run a weak-scaling test.
  • Overlap is not free. Larger $\delta$ means fewer iterations, but bigger local problems and more data to exchange. Production codes use small overlaps (a few cells) and rely on the coarse space.
  • A coarse space that misses the physics. For coefficients that jump by orders of magnitude (e.g. porous media), piecewise bilinear coarse functions are not enough: the coarse space must contain the "almost-null" modes of the operator. This is an active research topic (spectral coarse spaces such as GenEO).

Non-overlapping methods: the interface problem

Now cut the domain without overlap. The unknowns split into the interiors $I_1,\dots,I_K$ of the boxes and the interface $\Gamma$ (the vertices on the cut lines). Two interior unknowns of different subdomains are never neighbours in the mesh, so the matrix entry between them is zero. Number the interiors first and the interface last, and the matrix takes a block "arrow" shape:

$$\begin{pmatrix} A_{I_1I_1} & & & A_{I_1\Gamma}\\ & \ddots & & \vdots\\ & & A_{I_KI_K} & A_{I_K\Gamma}\\ A_{\Gamma I_1} & \cdots & A_{\Gamma I_K} & A_{\Gamma\Gamma} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf u_{I_1}\\ \vdots\\ \mathbf u_{I_K}\\ \mathbf u_\Gamma\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf b_{I_1}\\ \vdots\\ \mathbf b_{I_K}\\ \mathbf b_\Gamma\end{pmatrix}.$$

Solve each block row $k$ for the interior: $\mathbf u_{I_k} = A_{I_kI_k}^{-1}(\mathbf b_{I_k} - A_{I_k\Gamma}\mathbf u_\Gamma)$. Insert this into the last row and collect the terms with $\mathbf u_\Gamma$. This gives the interface problem

$$S\,\mathbf u_\Gamma = \mathbf g, \qquad\text{where}\qquad \begin{aligned} S &= A_{\Gamma\Gamma} - \sum_{k=1}^{K} A_{\Gamma I_k}A_{I_kI_k}^{-1}A_{I_k\Gamma},\\ \mathbf g &= \mathbf b_\Gamma - \sum_{k=1}^{K} A_{\Gamma I_k}A_{I_kI_k}^{-1}\mathbf b_{I_k}. \end{aligned}$$

$S$ is the Schur complement of the interiors. Once $\mathbf u_\Gamma$ is known, the interiors follow from $K$ independent Dirichlet solves.

This is the static condensation of chapter 9, with the elements there replaced by whole subdomains here. Hybridization eliminated the element interiors and kept a global problem for the multipliers on the mesh skeleton. Here we eliminate the subdomain interiors and keep a global problem on the subdomain skeleton $\Gamma$. Mechanical engineers call this substructuring. $S$ inherits symmetry and positive definiteness from $A$. It is dense (every interface unknown of a subdomain talks to every other one through the interior), so in practice it is never formed. CG only needs products $S\mathbf x$, and each one costs one Dirichlet solve per subdomain, all in parallel.

What $S$ means: the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map

Take interface values $\lambda$ and compute $S\lambda$ step by step. (1) Extend $\lambda$ into every subdomain by solving $-\Delta u_k = 0$ with $u_k = \lambda$ on $\Gamma$ (this is $-A_{I_kI_k}^{-1}A_{I_k\Gamma}\lambda$). (2) Evaluate the residual of the interface equations, $A_{\Gamma\Gamma}\lambda + \sum_k A_{\Gamma I_k}\mathbf u_{I_k}$. By the integration by parts that produced the weak form (chapter 2), the residual on $\Gamma$ of a discrete harmonic function is its (weak) normal flux $\int_\Gamma \partial_{n} u\,\varphi_i\,\mathrm ds$. So $S$ maps Dirichlet data on $\Gamma$ to the sum of the Neumann data (fluxes) of the subdomains. In the continuous setting this is the Steklov–Poincaré operator. Splitting $A = \sum_k A^{(k)}$ into contributions from the elements of each subdomain gives $S = \sum_k R_{\Gamma_k}^TS^{(k)}R_{\Gamma_k}$, where $S^{(k)}$ is the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map of subdomain $k$ alone (tested). The interface equation $S\mathbf u_\Gamma = \mathbf g$ says the fluxes from both sides balance: what leaves one subdomain enters its neighbour. It is a conservation law (chapter 1) on the interface.

Why $S$ is better conditioned than $A$. Put a wave $\cos(k y)$ on a straight interface. Its harmonic extension into a half-plane is $e^{-|k|x}\cos(ky)$, with normal derivative $|k|\cos(ky)$: the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map multiplies mode $k$ by $|k|$, while the Laplacian multiplies it by $k^2$. On a mesh the frequencies range from $k\sim 1$ to $k \sim 1/h$, so

$$\kappa(A) \sim h^{-2}, \qquad \kappa(S) \sim h^{-1}.$$

The widget computes both. For our matrix $\kappa(A) = \cot^2(\pi h/2)$ exactly (it is the 5-point stencil, whose eigenvalues are known), the largest eigenvalue of $S$ stays bounded and the smallest one is proportional to $h$.

The Dirichlet–Neumann method

For two subdomains, the transmission conditions on $\Gamma$ are: the values agree ($u_1 = u_2$) and the fluxes balance ($\partial_{n_1}u_1 + \partial_{n_2}u_2 = 0$, with outward normals $n_1 = -n_2$). The Dirichlet–Neumann method gives one condition to each subdomain:

  1. Dirichlet solve in $\Omega_1$: $-\Delta u_1 = f$ in $\Omega_1$, $u_1 = \lambda^n$ on $\Gamma$.
  2. Neumann solve in $\Omega_2$: $-\Delta u_2 = f$ in $\Omega_2$, $\partial_{n_2}u_2 = -\partial_{n_1}u_1$ on $\Gamma$ (receive the flux computed in step 1).
  3. Relaxation: $\lambda^{n+1} = \theta\, u_2|_\Gamma + (1-\theta)\,\lambda^n$, with $0 < \theta \le 1$.

In matrix form, step 1 computes $\mathbf u_{I_1}$ from $\lambda^n$, and step 2 solves the local Neumann matrix $A^{(2)}$ of $\Omega_2$ with the interface residual of $\Omega_1$ as right-hand side. Eliminating the interiors as above (do it once on paper!) shows that $S_2\,\mathbf u_\Gamma^{(2)} = \mathbf g - S_1\lambda^n$, so

$$\lambda^{n+1} = \lambda^n + \theta\,S_2^{-1}\big(\mathbf g - S\lambda^n\big), \qquad S = S_1 + S_2 .$$

This is a preconditioned Richardson iteration for the interface problem, with the preconditioner $S_2^{-1}$, a Neumann solve. The error is multiplied by $I - \theta S_2^{-1}S = I - \theta(I + S_2^{-1}S_1)$. If $\mu$ runs through the eigenvalues of $S_2^{-1}S_1$ (real and positive), the contraction factor is

$$\rho(\theta) = \max_\mu\,\big|1 - \theta(1+\mu)\big|, \qquad \text{smallest for}\quad \theta_{\rm opt} = \frac{2}{2+\mu_{\min}+\mu_{\max}} .$$
  • Symmetric split (interface in the middle): $S_1 = S_2$, all $\mu = 1$, and $\theta = \tfrac12$ gives the exact interface values after one step (tested).
  • High frequencies don't see the geometry. A rapidly oscillating interface mode decays within a short distance of $\Gamma$, so both subdomains look like half-planes to it: $\mu\approx1$ and the factor is $|1-2\theta|$. With $\theta = 1$ (no relaxation) these modes are never damped. For the lowest mode $\sin(\pi y)$ on strips of widths $L_1, L_2$, the Dirichlet-to-Neumann maps are $\pi\coth(\pi L_i)$, so $\mu \approx \coth(\pi L_1)/\coth(\pi L_2)$. For $L_1 = \tfrac14$ this is $\approx 1.50$, and the widget computes $\mu_{\max} \approx 1.498$. Then $\theta = 1$ even diverges ($\rho = \mu_{\max} > 1$), while $\theta = \tfrac12$ converges with $\rho \approx 0.25$ (all verified in the tests).

Neumann–Neumann, FETI and BDDC

Dirichlet–Neumann treats the subdomains unequally and works best for two of them. With many subdomains, the standard approach is to precondition $S$ with the local Neumann problems of all subdomains. This is the Neumann–Neumann preconditioner

$$M_{\rm NN}^{-1} = \sum_{k} R_{\Gamma_k}^T D_k \big(S^{(k)}\big)^{-1} D_k R_{\Gamma_k},$$

where $D_k$ are weights that form a partition of unity on $\Gamma$ (e.g. $\tfrac12$ on an edge shared by two subdomains). Since $S = \sum_k R_{\Gamma_k}^TS^{(k)}R_{\Gamma_k}$, this "sum of inverses of the pieces" is a natural approximation of the inverse of the sum.

Floating subdomains. A subdomain that does not touch the Dirichlet boundary (the centre box of a $3\times3$ partition) has a pure Neumann problem. Its solution is only determined up to a constant, and indeed $S^{(k)}\mathbf 1 = 0$: $S^{(k)}$ is singular (tested, and shown in the Schur widget). The local problems can then only be solved if the right-hand side has zero mean, i.e. if the net flux into the subdomain balances its sources. Enforcing these compatibility conditions for all floating subdomains at once couples them: it is a small global problem. So the coarse space appears by necessity in non-overlapping methods:

  • Balancing Neumann–Neumann (Mandel, 1993) adds a coarse correction built from the constants of each subdomain.
  • FETI (Finite Element Tearing and Interconnecting; Farhat & Roux, 1991) tears the mesh apart along $\Gamma$, duplicates the interface unknowns and glues them back with Lagrange multipliers that enforce continuity (a saddle-point problem as in chapter 9). It then solves the dual problem for the multipliers, which physically are the interface fluxes. The null spaces of the floating subdomains give FETI's "natural" coarse problem.
  • FETI-DP and BDDC (Dohrmann, 2003) keep a few primal constraints continuous, typically the values at subdomain corners and edge averages. These form the coarse problem and make every local problem non-singular. The two methods turned out to have the same spectrum, except possibly for the eigenvalues 0 and 1 (Mandel, Dohrmann & Tezaur, 2005).

For these methods the theory gives $\kappa \le C\,\big(1 + \log(H/h)\big)^2$. This bound is independent of the number of subdomains, and it grows only logarithmically with the number of elements per subdomain. They are the workhorses of large-scale structural mechanics.

Summary

  • Domain decomposition = local solves on subdomains + an iteration that makes them agree. It is the standard way to parallelise implicit PDE solvers.
  • Schwarz (1870): alternate solves on overlapping subdomains. In 1D the error shrinks by $\rho = \alpha(1-\beta)/(\beta(1-\alpha))$ per iteration, and $\rho\to1$ as the overlap vanishes.
  • In matrix form a local solve is $\mathbf u \leftarrow \mathbf u + R_k^TA_k^{-1}R_k(\mathbf b - A\mathbf u)$. Multiplicative = block Gauss–Seidel, additive = block Jacobi. Additive and restricted additive Schwarz are used as preconditioners for CG and GMRES.
  • One-level methods move information one subdomain per iteration: $\kappa \lesssim 1 + 1/(H\delta)$, not scalable. A coarse space gives $\kappa \lesssim 1 + H/\delta$: iteration counts stay bounded as subdomains are added.
  • Non-overlapping methods condense the subdomain interiors (static condensation, as in hybridization) into the interface problem $S\mathbf u_\Gamma = \mathbf g$. $S$ is a Dirichlet-to-Neumann map with $\kappa(S)\sim h^{-1}$ instead of $h^{-2}$.
  • Dirichlet–Neumann needs relaxation ($\theta = \tfrac12$ damps all high-frequency interface modes). Neumann–Neumann, FETI and BDDC handle many subdomains. Floating subdomains make their local problems singular, and the coarse problem appears naturally.

Chapter 12 turns to the parallelism inside one processor: GPUs.
