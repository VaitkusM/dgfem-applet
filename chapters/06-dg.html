6Discontinuous Galerkin for conservation laws

High-order polynomials inside every cell, glued together only by numerical fluxes.

Finite volumes (chapter 5) store one number per cell and must reconstruct slopes from the neighbours to get beyond first order. The discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method instead stores a whole polynomial of degree $p$ in every cell and evolves all its coefficients. Cells talk to each other only through the numerical flux on their faces — exactly as in finite volumes. The result is a method that is high order, locally conservative, naturally upwind, works on any mesh, and keeps all heavy work inside the cells (which is why it loves GPUs, chapter 12).

From finite volumes to piecewise polynomials

A finite volume method knows only the cell averages $\bar u_k$. To compute a face flux to second order it guesses a slope from neighbouring averages (MUSCL), and higher orders need ever wider stencils. DG takes the opposite route: make the slope (and the curvature, and …) an unknown of the cell itself. The discrete solution $u_h$ is sought in the broken space

$$V_h^p = \big\{\, v : v|_{K_k} \text{ is a polynomial of degree} \le p \text{ for every } k \,\big\},$$

with no continuity requirement across faces. Each element owns $p+1$ coefficients; $p=0$ gives piecewise constants, i.e. exactly the finite volume unknowns. At every face $u_h$ has two values, $u^-$ from the left element and $u^+$ from the right one; their difference is the jump $\jump{u} = u^- - u^+$.

Graphics analogy

Continuous finite elements (chapter 3) are like an indexed triangle mesh: neighbouring triangles share vertices, so attributes (colours, normals) interpolate continuously across edges. DG is like a flat-shaded mesh with split vertices: every element owns private copies of its vertex data, so attributes may jump across edges — and all the per-element work is independent, which is exactly what a GPU likes.

Deriving the DG scheme

We want an equation for each element's polynomial. Take the conservation law, multiply by a test function $v$ that is a polynomial of degree $\le p$ on $K_k$ (and zero elsewhere), and integrate over the element:

$$\int_{K_k} u_t\, v \dd x + \int_{K_k} f(u)_x\, v \dd x = 0 .$$

Integrate the second term by parts (the 1D divergence theorem of chapter 0): $\int_{K_k} f_x v = \big[f v\big]_{x_{k-\frac12}}^{x_{k+\frac12}} - \int_{K_k} f\,v_x$. Writing $v(x^-_{k+\frac12})$ for the value of $v$ at the right end of $K_k$ and $v(x^+_{k-\frac12})$ at its left end,

$$\int_{K_k} u_t\, v \dd x - \int_{K_k} f(u)\, v_x \dd x + f\big(u(x_{k+\frac12})\big)\, v(x^-_{k+\frac12}) - f\big(u(x_{k-\frac12})\big)\, v(x^+_{k-\frac12}) = 0 .$$

So far this is exact (it is the weak form on one cell). Now replace $u$ by $u_h \in V_h^p$. Inside the element $f(u_h)$ is well defined, but at a face $u_h$ has two values, and $f(u(x_{k+\frac12}))$ is ambiguous. The cure is the same as in finite volumes: use a numerical flux $\hat F_{k+\frac12} = \hat F(u^-_{k+\frac12}, u^+_{k+\frac12})$ that combines both values.

The DG method (weak form)

Find $u_h(t) \in V_h^p$ such that on every element $K_k$ and for every polynomial $v$ of degree $\le p$

$$\int_{K_k} \partial_t u_h\, v \dd x \;=\; \underbrace{\int_{K_k} f(u_h)\, v_x \dd x}_{\text{volume term}} \;-\; \underbrace{\Big(\hat F_{k+\frac12}\, v(x^-_{k+\frac12}) - \hat F_{k-\frac12}\, v(x^+_{k-\frac12})\Big)}_{\text{surface (face) term}} .$$

Every element needs only its own polynomial and the traces of its two neighbours — a compact stencil whatever $p$ is. Popular fluxes (chapter 5), written with jumps and averages:

  • upwind (advection, $f = au$): $\hat F = a\avg{u} + \frac{|a|}2\jump{u}$, which equals $a u^-$ for $a > 0$ and $a u^+$ for $a<0$;
  • central: $\hat F = \avg{f(u)}$ — no upwinding at all;
  • Rusanov / local Lax–Friedrichs: $\hat F = \avg{f(u)} + \frac{\alpha}{2}\jump{u}$ with $\alpha = \max(|f'(u^-)|, |f'(u^+)|)$ (for advection it equals upwind).

Conservation for free

The constant $v = 1$ is an allowed test function. Then $v_x = 0$ and the scheme says

$$\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int_{K_k} u_h \dd x = -\big(\hat F_{k+\frac12} - \hat F_{k-\frac12}\big),$$

which is exactly the finite volume update for the cell average. What leaves $K_k$ through a face enters its neighbour, so the sum over all elements telescopes: the total mass $\int u_h\,\mathrm dx$ is conserved to round-off, for any $p$, any flux, linear or nonlinear. And for $p = 0$ the volume term vanishes completely: DG with $p = 0$ is the first-order finite volume method.

  • Look closely at the faces: the jumps $\jump{u}$ are tiny for smooth data — DG's discontinuity is a measure of its own error.
  • Square wave with $p = 3$, no limiter: overshoots and undershoots next to the jumps (Gibbs). Switch on the minmod limiter: monotone, but the corners round off.
  • Switch to the central flux: the semi-discrete scheme conserves the energy, so it decays only slightly (through the damping of the RK method), and the wiggles spread over the whole domain.

Reference element, bases and matrices

To compute, map each element to the reference element $[-1,1]$ via $x = x_{k-\frac12} + (\xi + 1)h/2$; then $\mathrm dx = \frac h2\,\mathrm d\xi$ and $v_x = \frac2h\, v_\xi$. Expand $u_h|_{K_k}(\xi, t) = \sum_{j=0}^{p} u_{k,j}(t)\,\phi_j(\xi)$ in a basis $\phi_0,\dots,\phi_p$ of polynomials of degree $\le p$. Two families are popular:

  • Modal: Legendre polynomials $\phi_j = P_j$. They are orthogonal, $\int_{-1}^1 P_iP_j\,\mathrm d\xi = \frac{2}{2j+1}\delta_{ij}$, so the mass matrix is diagonal. The coefficient $u_{k,0}$ is the cell average and $u_{k,1}$ the "slope" — handy for limiters.
  • Nodal: Lagrange polynomials $\phi_j = \ell_j$ through nodes $\xi_0,\dots,\xi_p$, so the coefficients are point values $u_{k,j} = u_h(\xi_j)$. We use Gauss–Lobatto–Legendre (GLL) nodes, which include the end points $\pm1$ (the face values are then simply the first and last coefficients) or Gauss–Legendre nodes (interior only).

Testing with $v = \phi_i$ for $i = 0,\dots,p$ turns the DG equation on element $K_k$ into $p+1$ ODEs:

$$\frac h2 \sum_{j} M_{ij}\, \frac{\mathrm d u_{k,j}}{\mathrm dt} = \int_{-1}^1 f(u_h)\, \phi_i'(\xi)\dd\xi \;-\; \phi_i(1)\,\hat F_{k+\frac12} + \phi_i(-1)\,\hat F_{k-\frac12}, \qquad M_{ij} = \int_{-1}^1 \phi_i\phi_j \dd\xi .$$

(The factors: $\int_{K_k} u_t v\,\mathrm dx = \frac h2\int u_t v\,\mathrm d\xi$ and $\int_{K_k} f v_x\,\mathrm dx = \int f\, v_\xi\,\mathrm d\xi$ because $\frac h2\cdot\frac2h = 1$.) For linear advection $f = au$ this is a matrix equation with the stiffness matrix $S_{ij} = \int_{-1}^1 \phi_i\,\phi_j'\,\mathrm d\xi$:

$$\frac h2\, M\,\frac{\mathrm d\mathbf u_k}{\mathrm dt} = a\,S^{\mathsf T}\mathbf u_k - \boldsymbol\phi(1)\,\hat F_{k+\frac12} + \boldsymbol\phi(-1)\,\hat F_{k-\frac12}.$$

Example, $p = 1$ with nodes $\pm1$: $\ell_0 = \frac{1-\xi}2$, $\ell_1 = \frac{1+\xi}2$, $\ell_0' = -\tfrac12$, $\ell_1' = \tfrac12$, and

$$M = \begin{pmatrix}\tfrac23 & \tfrac13\\[2pt] \tfrac13 & \tfrac23\end{pmatrix},\qquad S = \begin{pmatrix}-\tfrac12 & \tfrac12\\[2pt] -\tfrac12 & \tfrac12\end{pmatrix},\qquad D = \begin{pmatrix}-\tfrac12 & \tfrac12\\[2pt] -\tfrac12 & \tfrac12\end{pmatrix},$$

where $D_{ij} = \ell_j'(\xi_i)$ is the differentiation matrix (nodal values of $u$ ↦ nodal values of $u_\xi$). Note $S + S^{\mathsf T} = \mathrm{diag}(-1, 1)$: this is integration by parts, $\int (\phi_i\phi_j)' = [\phi_i\phi_j]_{-1}^{1}$, in matrix form. In general $S + S^{\mathsf T} = \boldsymbol\phi(1)\boldsymbol\phi(1)^{\mathsf T} - \boldsymbol\phi(-1)\boldsymbol\phi(-1)^{\mathsf T}$, which for GLL nodes and any $p$ is $\mathrm{diag}(-1, 0, \dots, 0, 1)$. This summation-by-parts property is the discrete reason why DG is stable.

Quadrature, lumping and aliasing

The integrals have to be computed by quadrature. Two strategies are common, and they are not the same method:

  1. Exact integration ("over-integration"). Use a Gauss rule with enough points: the mass matrix needs degree $2p$; the Burgers volume term $\int \tfrac12 u_h^2\,\ell_i'$ has degree $3p-1$. Our code uses $\lceil (3p+2)/2\rceil + 1$ Gauss points by default, enough for both.
  2. Collocation. Use the $p+1$ solution nodes themselves as quadrature points. With GLL nodes this is the classical DG spectral element method (DG-SEM). Then $M$ becomes diagonal, $M = \mathrm{diag}(w_0,\dots,w_p)$ — the lumped mass matrix — and the flux enters only through its nodal values $f(u_{k,j})$. The price: GLL with $p+1$ points is exact only up to degree $2p-1$, so the lumped $M$ is not the exact Gram matrix (it has the same row sums, $\int\ell_i$, but differs entry by entry).

For linear advection every volume integrand has degree $\le 2p-1$, so the only inexact ingredient of DG-SEM is the lumped mass matrix; both versions converge at the optimal rate $p+1$ (tested for $p = 1,\dots,4$). For a nonlinear flux collocation replaces $f(u_h)$ by its degree-$p$ interpolant: the part of $u_h^2$ of degree $p+1,\dots,2p$ is folded back onto low degrees. This is aliasing — the same phenomenon as sampling a signal below its Nyquist rate. Concretely: with the entropy-conservative flux $\hat F = \big((u^-)^2 + u^-u^+ + (u^+)^2\big)/6$ and exact integration, the semi-discrete Burgers energy $\frac12\|u_h\|^2$ is conserved exactly (take $v = u_h$: $\int \frac{u_h^2}2\,\partial_x u_h = [u_h^3/6]$, and the face terms cancel it); with GLL collocation the same flux produces a nonzero energy rate for generic, under-resolved data. Aliasing errors are harmless for well-resolved solutions but can destabilise under-resolved simulations; the cures are over-integration, filtering, or split forms.

Graphics analogy

Squaring a polynomial doubles its "frequency content", just like multiplying two textures. Evaluating the product only at the original sample points aliases the high frequencies into low ones — the moiré patterns of texture minification. Over-integration is supersampling.

The semi-discrete system and one update, dissected

Collecting all elements gives a large system of ODEs, $\frac{\mathrm d\mathbf u}{\mathrm dt} = L(\mathbf u)$ ("method of lines"). Because $M$ is a small $(p{+}1)\times(p{+}1)$ block per element, $L$ is cheap to evaluate: compute the face fluxes, then on each element $\frac{\mathrm d\mathbf u_k}{\mathrm dt} = \frac2h M^{-1}\big(\text{volume} - \text{surface}\big)$. We advance it with an explicit SSP Runge–Kutta method (SSP-RK3 of Shu & Osher by default).

The two parts of the right-hand side have intuitive roles. The volume term $\frac2h M^{-1}\int f(u_h)\,\phi_i'$ moves material around inside the element; its integral against $v = 1$ is zero, so it never changes the cell's mass. The surface term is a pair of "lifted" face corrections: it injects what flows in through the left face and removes what leaves through the right face, spread over the element by $M^{-1}$. Their sum is $\partial_t u_h$, which for smooth data approximates $-f(u)_x$.

Upwind vs central flux: L² stability

Why do we need upwinding? Take linear advection $f = au$ and test with $v = u_h$ itself (allowed: $u_h|_{K_k}$ is a polynomial of degree $\le p$). With exact integration, $\int_{K_k} a\,u_h\,\partial_x u_h = \frac a2\big[(u^-_{k+\frac12})^2 - (u^+_{k-\frac12})^2\big]$, so

$$\frac12\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int_{K_k} u_h^2 \dd x = \frac a2\big(u^-_{k+\frac12}\big)^2 - \frac a2\big(u^+_{k-\frac12}\big)^2 - \hat F_{k+\frac12}\,u^-_{k+\frac12} + \hat F_{k-\frac12}\,u^+_{k-\frac12}.$$

Sum over all elements (periodic domain) and collect the terms belonging to one face, where $u^-$ comes from the left element and $u^+$ from the right one:

$$\frac a2\big((u^-)^2 - (u^+)^2\big) - \hat F\,(u^- - u^+) = \jump{u}\big(a\avg{u} - \hat F\big).$$

For a flux of the form $\hat F = a\avg{u} + \frac\lambda2\jump{u}$ this is $-\frac\lambda2\jump{u}^2$, hence

$$\frac12\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\|u_h\|_{L^2}^2 = -\sum_{\text{faces}} \frac{\lambda}{2}\,\jump{u}^2 .$$

Upwind ($\lambda = |a|$): the energy decreases, and the dissipation is proportional to the squared jumps. Central ($\lambda = 0$): the energy is exactly conserved.

The upwind flux damps exactly what DG cannot represent well — the jumps. The central flux is "stable" in the sense that the energy does not grow, but nothing damps the high-frequency garbage; its convergence is irregular (we observe at least order $p$, sometimes $p+1$, depending on $p$ and $N$), while upwind gives a clean $p+1$. The same argument works with GLL collocation, in the discrete norm $\mathbf u^{\mathsf T}M_{\text{lumped}}\mathbf u$, thanks to summation by parts. Our tests check the matrix version: the symmetric part of $(h/2)M L$ is negative semidefinite for upwind and zero for central.

Spectrum and the CFL condition

For linear advection on a periodic mesh, $L$ is a matrix and its eigenvalues — the spectrum — decide whether an explicit time step is stable. One step of an RK method multiplies each eigen-component by $R(\lambda\Delta t)$, where $R$ is the method's stability function (a polynomial; e.g. $R(z) = 1 + z + z^2/2 + z^3/6$ for SSP-RK3). Stability requires $|R(\lambda\Delta t)| \le 1$ for every eigenvalue $\lambda$: the scaled spectrum $\lambda\Delta t$ must fit inside the stability region $\{z : |R(z)| \le 1\}$.

The upwind eigenvalues lie in the left half plane (energy decreases), the central ones on the imaginary axis. Their size grows with $p$, so the time step must shrink, roughly like $h/(2p+1)$. Precisely, with the CFL number $\nu = |a|\Delta t/h$ (element width $h$), the largest stable values computed from the spectrum (Bloch analysis, $N \to \infty$) are:

The lumped (GLL) mass matrix gives a markedly larger stable time step (more than 1.5× for $p = 1,\dots,4$ in our tests) — one reason DG-SEM is popular.

Shocks, Gibbs oscillations and slope limiting

A polynomial cannot jump. Near a discontinuity the best degree-$p$ fit overshoots (Gibbs phenomenon), and for nonlinear problems these oscillations can grow or create unphysical values (negative densities in gas dynamics). Godunov's theorem (chapter 5) says that a linear scheme that never creates new extrema is at most first order accurate, so we need a nonlinear device: a limiter. The classical choice for DG is the slope limiter of Cockburn and Shu, applied after every RK stage:

  1. Detect troubled elements. Let $\bar u_k$ be the cell average and $\Delta^\pm = \pm(\bar u_{k\pm1} - \bar u_k)$. The element is fine if the deviations of its face values from the average, $u^-_{k+\frac12} - \bar u_k$ and $\bar u_k - u^+_{k-\frac12}$, are not larger than (and have the same sign as) the neighbouring differences: $\operatorname{minmod}(u^-_{k+\frac12} - \bar u_k, \Delta^-, \Delta^+) = u^-_{k+\frac12} - \bar u_k$, and similarly on the left.
  2. Limit. In troubled elements keep only the linear part $\bar u_k + s_k\,\xi$ and replace its slope by $s_k \leftarrow \operatorname{minmod}(s_k, \tfrac12\Delta^-, \tfrac12\Delta^+)$, i.e. $u_x \leftarrow \operatorname{minmod}(u_x, \Delta^-/h, \Delta^+/h)$, the MUSCL minmod slope.

Here $\operatorname{minmod}(a,b,c)$ is the smallest in magnitude if all three have the same sign, else $0$. The cell average is never touched, so conservation is preserved. Pure minmod also flattens smooth extrema (it cannot tell a peak from a shock), which degrades accuracy there. The TVB fix (Shu 1987) leaves a quantity alone if it is small, $|a| \le M h^2$, with a user constant $M$ of the size of $|u_{xx}|$ at extrema.

What goes wrong
  • Limiter everywhere: with $M = 0$ the limiter fires at every smooth extremum, and a sine wave slowly turns into a trapezoid. Use TVB ($M>0$) or a better troubled-cell indicator.
  • Too large a time step: DG's CFL limit shrinks like $1/(2p+1)$. Using the finite-volume habit $\nu \approx 0.9$ with $p = 3$ explodes within a few steps.
  • Aliasing: under-resolved nonlinear problems with collocation can blow up even without shocks; over-integrate or filter.
  • Central flux: energy-conserving does not mean accurate — without dissipation the high-frequency errors never decay.

Two dimensions: tensor products on quads

On a Cartesian mesh of rectangles the reference element is the square $[-1,1]^2$ and the natural space is $Q_p$: polynomials of degree $\le p$ in each variable separately, with the tensor-product basis $\ell_i(\xi)\,\ell_j(\eta)$ and nodes at the tensor GLL points $(\xi_i, \eta_j)$ — $(p+1)^2$ per element. For $u_t + \nabla\cdot\mathbf F(u) = 0$ the DG equation on an element $K$ is

$$\int_K \partial_t u_h\, v \dd\mathbf x = \int_K \mathbf F(u_h)\cdot\nabla v \dd\mathbf x - \int_{\partial K} \hat F(u^-, u^+, \mathbf n)\, v \dd s,$$

with the numerical flux now taken in the direction of the outward normal $\mathbf n$. With GLL collocation (DG-SEM) everything factorises: the volume term becomes the 1D differentiation matrix applied along every grid line in $x$ and in $y$, and each face is a 1D set of $p+1$ points where neighbours meet — exactly the 1D scheme, applied line by line, like a separable image filter. Faces of a structured grid with periodic wrap-around are found by index arithmetic; on unstructured meshes one needs a face-to-element connectivity table instead.

Summary

  • DG approximates in the broken space $V_h^p$: a polynomial per element, discontinuous across faces.
  • Test with polynomials on one element, integrate by parts, replace the face flux by a numerical flux: volume term + surface term.
  • Testing with $v = 1$ gives the finite volume update: local conservation. $p = 0$ is first-order FV.
  • Upwind flux: $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\frac12\|u_h\|^2 = -\sum\frac{|a|}2\jump{u}^2 \le 0$; central flux: energy conserved, no damping.
  • Smooth solutions: error $O(h^{p+1})$. Explicit time steps: $\nu = |a|\Delta t/h \lesssim 1/(2p+1)$.
  • Exact integration vs GLL collocation (lumped mass, DG-SEM): equal accuracy for linear problems, aliasing for nonlinear ones.
  • Discontinuities need limiters (minmod/TVB), which keep cell averages and hence conservation.
