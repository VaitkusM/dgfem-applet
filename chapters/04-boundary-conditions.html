4Strong vs weak boundary conditions

Forcing boundary values into the unknowns, or asking the equations to respect them: elimination, penalties, Nitsche's method, and inflow/outflow conditions for transport.

A PDE has infinitely many solutions until we say what happens at the boundary. In chapter 2 the Dirichlet condition $u = g$ was essential: built into the function space. That is the strong way. It is simple on a mesh that follows the boundary, but it breaks down for discontinuous Galerkin methods, for meshes that do not fit the geometry (chapter 11), for high-order modal bases and for many complicated conditions. The weak way imposes the condition through extra terms in the equations — and the boundary term we discarded in chapter 2 comes back.

Strong imposition

With nodal (Lagrange) basis functions, the coefficient of a boundary node is the value of $u_h$ there. So we simply set $U_j = g(\mathbf x_j)$ for every boundary node $j$ and keep only the equations of interior nodes. Writing the unknowns as interior (I) and boundary (B) blocks,

$$\begin{pmatrix} A_{II} & A_{IB}\\ A_{BI} & A_{BB}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf U_I\\ \mathbf U_B\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf b_I\\ \mathbf b_B\end{pmatrix} \quad\longrightarrow\quad A_{II}\,\mathbf U_I = \mathbf b_I - A_{IB}\,\mathbf g_B .$$

The boundary rows are dropped (their test functions do not vanish on $\Gamma_D$), and the known boundary columns move to the right-hand side — this lifting keeps $A_{II}$ symmetric positive definite. (A popular shortcut, replacing boundary rows by identity rows, gives the same solution but a non-symmetric matrix unless the columns are eliminated too.) This is what chapter 3 did.

Strong imposition is exact at the nodes and costs nothing — but it needs (i) nodes on the boundary, (ii) basis functions whose coefficients are point values, and (iii) a condition that can be written as "unknown = value". DG, unfitted meshes, spline bases and conditions like "no flow through a curved wall" violate at least one of these.

The penalty method

The simplest weak alternative: keep all nodes as unknowns, and punish violations of the boundary condition by adding a large spring term:

$$\int_\Omega\nabla u_h\cdot\nabla v\dd\mathbf x + \sum_{E\subset\partial\Omega}\frac{\gamma}{h_E}\int_E (u_h - g)\,v\dd s = \int_\Omega f v\dd\mathbf x .$$

It is inconsistent: the exact solution does not satisfy this equation, because the integration by parts produces the boundary term $-\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_n u\,v\dd s$, which is missing here. The penalty method really solves the Robin problem $\partial_n u + \frac{\gamma}{h}(u - g) = 0$, i.e. $u - g = -\frac h\gamma\,\partial_n u$ on the boundary: an $O(h/\gamma)$ error in the boundary condition. That caps the $L^2$ convergence rate at 1 (for any polynomial degree) unless $\gamma$ grows with refinement — which ruins the condition number.

Nitsche's method

Nitsche (1971) fixed the inconsistency by keeping the boundary term instead of dropping it. Start from the integration by parts with test functions that do not vanish on the boundary — valid for the exact solution:

$$\int_\Omega\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\dd\mathbf x - \int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_n u\,v\dd s = \int_\Omega f v\dd\mathbf x .$$

Now add two terms that are zero for the exact solution (because $u - g = 0$ on the boundary), so consistency is preserved:

  • a symmetrising term $-\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_n v\,(u - g)\dd s$, which makes the bilinear form symmetric;
  • a penalty term $+\sum_E\frac{\gamma}{h_E}\int_E (u - g)\,v\dd s$, which makes it positive.

Symmetric Nitsche: find $u_h\in V_h$ (no boundary constraint!) with $a_h(u_h, v) = \ell_h(v)$ for all $v\in V_h$, where

$$\begin{aligned} a_h(u,v) &= \int_\Omega\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\dd\mathbf x - \int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_n u\,v\dd s - \int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_n v\,u\dd s + \sum_E\frac{\gamma}{h_E}\int_E u\,v\dd s,\\ \ell_h(v) &= \int_\Omega f\,v\dd\mathbf x - \int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_n v\,g\dd s + \sum_E\frac{\gamma}{h_E}\int_E g\,v\dd s . \end{aligned}$$

Consistency means Galerkin orthogonality still holds, so the error is again a best-approximation error: optimal rates $h^{p}$ in $H^1$ and $h^{p+1}$ in $L^2$ — unlike the penalty method.

Why must $\gamma$ be large enough? Testing with $v = u_h$:

$$a_h(v,v) = \|\nabla v\|^2_{L^2(\Omega)} - 2\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_n v\,v\dd s + \sum_E\frac{\gamma}{h_E}\|v\|^2_{L^2(E)} .$$

The middle term has no sign. For polynomials there is an inverse trace inequality $h_E\|\partial_n v\|^2_{L^2(E)}\le C_I\|\nabla v\|^2_{L^2(K)}$ (a polynomial cannot have a large normal derivative on an edge without having a large gradient inside the element; $C_I$ grows like $p^2$ and depends on the element shape, not on $h$). With Young's inequality $2ab \le \varepsilon a^2 + b^2/\varepsilon$ one gets $a_h(v,v) \ge \tfrac12\|\nabla v\|^2 + \sum_E\frac{\gamma - 2C_I}{h_E}\|v\|_E^2$: the method is coercive (stable) as soon as $\gamma > 2C_I$, a fixed number independent of $h$. Below that threshold the matrix can have negative eigenvalues — demonstrated below.

Non-symmetric Nitsche flips the sign of the symmetrising term ($+\int\partial_n v\,u$, and $+\int\partial_n v\,g$ on the right). Then the two boundary terms cancel in $a_h(v,v)$ and the method is coercive for any $\gamma\ge0$ (Burman 2012 even shows $\gamma = 0$ works). The price is a non-symmetric matrix and the loss of adjoint consistency, which can reduce the $L^2$ rate.

Big picture

Nitsche's method is the template for all weakly coupled methods in this course: applied on every interior face it becomes the symmetric interior penalty DG method (chapter 8); applied on a boundary that cuts through elements it becomes CutFEM (chapter 11); applied on a shifted boundary it becomes the shifted boundary method (chapter 11). It can also be derived as a stabilised Lagrange-multiplier method (Stenberg 1995), which links it to the hybridization ideas of chapter 9.

Try this
  • Set $\gamma = 1$ with symmetric Nitsche: the read-out reports a negative eigenvalue and the solution is garbage. Raise $\gamma$ until it turns positive.
  • Run the convergence study for "penalty only": the $L^2$ rate is about 1 instead of 2 (P1) or 3 (P2).
  • Very large $\gamma$ ($10^4$): Nitsche behaves like strong imposition — but the matrix becomes badly conditioned. A moderate $\gamma\approx 10p^2$ is the usual choice.

Boundary conditions for transport

For the advection equation $u_t + a u_x = 0$ with $a > 0$ (chapter 1), characteristics enter the domain at $x = 0$ (the inflow boundary) and leave at $x = 1$ (the outflow boundary). The solution inside is completely determined by the initial data and the inflow data; prescribing anything at the outflow over-determines the problem. In 2D the inflow part of the boundary is where $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf n < 0$; for the Euler equations, the number of conditions equals the number of characteristic speeds pointing into the domain (e.g. a subsonic outflow needs one condition, typically the pressure).

Finite volume and DG methods impose boundary conditions weakly through the numerical flux: on a boundary face the flux is $\hat F(u^-, u^+)$ with an exterior ghost state $u^+$. At the inflow $u^+ = g$; at the outflow one may offer any value — an upwind flux simply ignores it, because it only uses the upwind (interior) state. This is the hyperbolic analogue of Nitsche's method: the flux decides which data is used. Overwriting boundary cells (strong imposition) works at the inflow but produces a wrong boundary value at the outflow.

What goes wrong
  • Symmetric Nitsche with too small $\gamma$ — or with $\gamma$ not scaled by $1/h_E$ — is unstable. The safe threshold grows like $p^2$ and with element distortion.
  • The penalty method converges, but to the wrong boundary condition at a rate limited to $O(h)$.
  • Prescribing data at an outflow boundary of a hyperbolic problem is an ill-posed over-specification; in practice it shows up as spurious boundary layers or reflections.
