9Mixed methods & hybridization

Make the flux an unknown of its own, understand the saddle point this creates, and then break the problem apart so that only a small symmetric positive definite system on the mesh edges remains.

In chapter 1 the flux was the star of the show: conservation laws are statements about fluxes through boundaries. Yet the methods of chapters 3 and 8 compute only $u$; the flux $-\nabla u$ is obtained afterwards by differentiation, and it is neither continuous across elements nor exactly conservative. Mixed methods compute the flux directly and conserve it exactly, cell by cell. The price is a saddle-point problem. Hybridization removes that price — and, applied to DG, gives the HDG method, whose global system is much smaller than that of interior penalty DG of the same degree.

Fluxes as primary unknowns

Split $-\Delta u = f$ into Fourier's law and the conservation law (chapter 1, with steady state and diffusivity 1):

$$\bsigma = -\nabla u \quad\text{(constitutive law)},\qquad \nabla\cdot\bsigma = f \quad\text{(conservation)},\qquad u = g \text{ on }\partial\Omega .$$

Why bother? In many applications the flux is the answer: the Darcy velocity of groundwater or oil in a porous rock, the heat flux through a wall, the current density in a conductor. Feeding a velocity field into a transport solver (chapters 5–6) requires that it conserves mass exactly in every cell — otherwise a tracer is created or destroyed numerically. A gradient computed from CG is not conservative: $\int_{\partial K}(-\nabla u_h)\cdot\n \neq \int_K f$ in general (for $P_1$ elements $\nabla u_h$ is constant on each triangle, so its net outflow is exactly zero, whatever the source $f$ is).

Weak form. Multiply the constitutive law by a vector test function $\boldsymbol\tau$ and integrate the $\nabla u$ term by parts (chapter 2): $(\bsigma,\boldsymbol\tau) + (\nabla u,\boldsymbol\tau) = (\bsigma,\boldsymbol\tau) - (u,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\tau) + \langle u,\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\n\rangle_{\partial\Omega} = 0$. Multiply the conservation law by a scalar $v$. With $u = g$ on the boundary:

$$\begin{aligned} (\bsigma, \boldsymbol\tau) - (u, \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\tau) &= -\langle g, \boldsymbol\tau\cdot\n\rangle_{\partial\Omega} &&\text{for all }\boldsymbol\tau,\\ (\nabla\cdot\bsigma, v) &= (f, v) &&\text{for all } v. \end{aligned}$$

Notice the role reversal compared with chapters 2–4: the Dirichlet condition $u = g$ is now natural (it appears on the right-hand side, nothing has to be built into a space), while a Neumann condition $\bsigma\cdot\n = g_N$ would be essential. No derivatives of $u$ appear at all, so $u$ may be discontinuous: piecewise constants are fine. But $\nabla\cdot\bsigma$ must make sense.

Saddle points and the inf–sup condition

The mixed form is the optimality condition of the Lagrangian

$$\mathcal L(\bsigma, u) = \tfrac12(\bsigma,\bsigma) + \langle g, \bsigma\cdot\n\rangle_{\partial\Omega} - (u, \nabla\cdot\bsigma - f):$$

setting the derivative in direction $\boldsymbol\tau$ to zero gives the first equation, in direction $v$ the second. Read it as: among all fluxes that satisfy the conservation law $\nabla\cdot\bsigma = f$, find the one with the smallest energy; $u$ is the Lagrange multiplier of the conservation constraint. $\mathcal L$ is convex in $\bsigma$ and linear in $u$, so the solution is a saddle point — a minimum in $\bsigma$ and a maximum in $u$. After discretisation (basis functions for $\bsigma_h$ and $u_h$) the linear system is

$$\begin{pmatrix} M & -B^T\\ -B & 0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol\Sigma\\ \mathbf U\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-\mathbf G\\ -\mathbf F\end{pmatrix},\qquad M_{ij} = (\boldsymbol\varphi_j,\boldsymbol\varphi_i),\quad B_{Kj} = \int_K\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\varphi_j$$

(the second block row multiplied by $-1$ to make the matrix symmetric). $M$ is SPD, but the whole matrix is indefinite: if $B^T$ is injective it has exactly as many positive eigenvalues as flux unknowns and as many negative ones as $u$ unknowns. Conjugate gradients do not apply; we use a direct solver with pivoting.

When is it invertible? Eliminating $\boldsymbol\Sigma = M^{-1}(B^T\mathbf U - \mathbf G)$ leaves $BM^{-1}B^T\,\mathbf U = \dots$, which is invertible exactly when $B^T$ is injective: every discrete $u_h \ne 0$ must be “seen” by some flux, $(u_h, \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\tau_h)\ne 0$. Its quantitative, mesh-independent version is the inf–sup (LBB) condition

$$\inf_{u_h\ne0}\ \sup_{\boldsymbol\tau_h\ne0}\ \frac{(u_h,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\tau_h)}{\|u_h\|\,\|\boldsymbol\tau_h\|_{H(\mathrm{div})}} \;\ge\; \beta > 0 \quad\text{with } \beta \text{ independent of } h .$$

Intuition: the flux space must be rich enough to control every pressure-like mode of $u_h$. A blunt counter-example is a dimension count. Take continuous piecewise-linear fluxes ($2(N+1)^2$ unknowns on an $N\times N$ grid of $2N^2$ triangles) with discontinuous piecewise-linear $u_h$ ($6N^2$ unknowns). For $N\ge 2$, $2(N+1)^2 < 6N^2$, so $B^T$ maps a larger space into a smaller one and cannot be injective: the matrix is singular, whatever we do. Even when the counts are fine, “natural-looking” equal-order pairs can fail more subtly — for the Stokes equations the continuous $P_1$–$P_1$ velocity–pressure pair admits spurious checkerboard pressures (Brezzi & Fortin). The Raviart–Thomas pair below is designed so that $\nabla\cdot$ maps the flux space onto the $u$-space; it satisfies the inf–sup condition.

Raviart–Thomas elements: the unknowns are fluxes through edges

What continuity is needed? For a piecewise polynomial $\bsigma_h$, the divergence theorem on each triangle shows that $\nabla\cdot\bsigma_h$ is a function (no delta functions on edges) exactly when the normal component $\bsigma_h\cdot\n$ is continuous across every edge — what flows out of one triangle flows into the next. The tangential component may jump. This is the space $H(\mathrm{div})$, and it is precisely the physics of conservation.

RT0 on one triangle consists of the fields $\boldsymbol\varphi(\mathbf x) = \mathbf a + b\,\mathbf x$ with $\mathbf a\in\R^2$, $b\in\R$: three parameters. On an edge (a line $\mathbf x\cdot\n = c$) the normal component $\mathbf a\cdot\n + b\,c$ is constant, and $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\varphi = 2b$ is constant. A natural basis has one function per edge. For edge $E$ of triangle $K$ with opposite vertex $\mathbf x_E^K$,

$$\boldsymbol\varphi_E\big|_K(\mathbf x) = s_{K,E}\,\frac{|E|}{2|K|}\,\big(\mathbf x - \mathbf x_E^K\big),\qquad s_{K,E} = \begin{cases}+1 & \n_E \text{ points out of } K,\\ -1 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$
  • On the two other edges of $K$, $\mathbf x - \mathbf x_E^K$ runs along the edge (both end at $\mathbf x_E^K$), so $\boldsymbol\varphi_E\cdot\n = 0$ there.
  • On $E$, $(\mathbf x - \mathbf x_E^K)\cdot\n_K$ equals the height of $K$ over $E$, i.e. $2|K|/|E|$; hence $\boldsymbol\varphi_E\cdot\n_E = 1$, from both triangles sharing $E$.
  • $\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\varphi_E|_K = s_{K,E}\,|E|/|K|$, so $\int_K\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\varphi_E = s_{K,E}\,|E|$.

The RT0 function of edge $E$ is a scaled radial field emanating from the opposite vertex.

The coefficient of $\boldsymbol\varphi_E$ in $\bsigma_h = \sum_E\Sigma_E\boldsymbol\varphi_E$ is therefore the normal flux density $\bsigma_h\cdot\n_E$ on $E$, and the total flux through $E$ is $\Sigma_E|E|$. One unknown per edge, shared by the two triangles: normal continuity holds by construction. Each triangle needs the sign $s_{K,E}$ because one global orientation $\n_E$ per edge is outward for one neighbour and inward for the other — the classic bookkeeping of oriented edges in a half-edge data structure.

Exact conservation. Testing the second equation with the indicator function of one triangle gives $\int_K\nabla\cdot\bsigma_h = \sum_{E\subset\partial K}s_{K,E}|E|\Sigma_E = \int_K f$: the net outflow of every triangle equals its source exactly (up to round-off and the quadrature of $f$). Accuracy: $\|u - u_h\|$ and $\|\bsigma - \bsigma_h\|$ both decrease like $h$; the distance of $u_h$ to the element means of $u$ even like $h^2$ (superconvergence).

Hybridization: tear the fluxes apart, glue them with multipliers

The saddle-point system is indefinite and couples everything. The trick of hybridization (Fraeijs de Veubeke 1965; Arnold & Brezzi 1985) is counter-intuitive: make the space larger. Give every triangle its own three flux unknowns $\sigma_{K,k}$ (normal flux on its local edge $k$ w.r.t. its own outward normal), with no continuity between neighbours — a broken RT0 space. Then re-impose normal continuity weakly with a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ living on the edges:

$$\begin{aligned} (\bsigma_h,\boldsymbol\tau)_K - (u_h,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\tau)_K + \langle\lambda_h,\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\n_K\rangle_{\partial K} &= 0 &&\forall\,\boldsymbol\tau\in RT_0(K),\\ (\nabla\cdot\bsigma_h, v)_K &= (f, v)_K &&\forall\, v\in P_0(K),\\ \textstyle\sum_{K\ni E}\langle\bsigma_h\cdot\n_K, \mu\rangle_E &= 0 &&\forall\,\mu\in P_0(E), \end{aligned}$$

the first two lines on every triangle $K$, the last one on every interior edge $E$; on boundary edges $\lambda_h = \frac1{|E|}\int_E g$.

The last line says $\bsigma_{K^-}\cdot\n_E = \bsigma_{K^+}\cdot\n_E$: the torn fluxes agree after all, and the solution coincides with the mixed RT0 solution (to round-off in the widget below and in the tests). What is $\lambda$? For the exact solution, integrating $(\bsigma,\boldsymbol\tau)_K = -(\nabla u,\boldsymbol\tau)_K$ by parts gives $(\bsigma,\boldsymbol\tau)_K - (u,\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\tau)_K + \langle u,\boldsymbol\tau\cdot\n_K\rangle_{\partial K} = 0$. Comparing with the first line, $\lambda_h$ takes the place of $u$ on the element boundary: the multiplier is an approximation of $u$ on the edges (our tests measure $\lambda_E - \frac1{|E|}\int_E u = O(h^2)$ in a mesh-weighted edge norm).

Static condensation

Now the magic. For one triangle collect $\mathbf x_K = (\sigma_{K,0},\sigma_{K,1},\sigma_{K,2},u_K)$. The first two lines are a $4\times4$ system that only involves $K$ and the three multipliers on its edges:

$$D_K\,\mathbf x_K = \mathbf r_K - C_K\boldsymbol\lambda_K,\qquad D_K = \begin{pmatrix}A_K & -B_K^T\\ -B_K & 0\end{pmatrix},$$ $$C_K = \begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{diag}(|E_0|,|E_1|,|E_2|)\\ 0\;\;0\;\;0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathbf r_K = \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf 0\\ -\int_K f\end{pmatrix},$$

where $A_K$ is the $3\times3$ RT0 mass matrix of $K$ and $B_K = (|E_0|, |E_1|, |E_2|)$ holds the integrals of the divergences.

$D_K$ is a tiny invertible matrix, so the local solver $\mathbf x_K = D_K^{-1}(\mathbf r_K - C_K\boldsymbol\lambda_K)$ expresses all element unknowns through the multipliers. Inserting this into the continuity equations $\sum_K C_K^T\mathbf x_K = 0$ leaves a system for $\lambda$ alone:

$$H\boldsymbol\lambda = \sum_K C_K^TD_K^{-1}\mathbf r_K,\qquad H = \sum_K C_K^T D_K^{-1}C_K .$$

In block form this is Gaussian elimination of the element block from $\begin{pmatrix} D & C\\ C^T & 0\end{pmatrix}$, i.e. the Schur complement $0 - C^TD^{-1}C = -H$. Because $D$ is block diagonal, $D^{-1}$ is computed one $4\times4$ block at a time — that is static condensation. The result is remarkable:

  • $H$ is symmetric positive definite (locally $C_K^TD_K^{-1}C_K$ is positive semidefinite with the constants as kernel; the Dirichlet edges remove them), so CG and all SPD machinery are back;
  • it has one unknown per interior edge and each row couples an edge only to the other edges of its two triangles (at most 5 non-zeros per row);
  • all element work (forming $D_K^{-1}$, recovering $\mathbf x_K$ afterwards) is embarrassingly parallel.
Graphics analogy

A two-pass renderer. The expensive global pass runs only on the wireframe (the skeleton of edges); then every triangle is filled independently, like a fragment shader that only needs the values on its own three edges. Chapter 10 shows that domain decomposition uses the very same Schur-complement idea with whole subdomains instead of single triangles.

HDG: hybridizing a discontinuous Galerkin method

The same tearing-and-gluing works for DG. Take the LDG idea from chapter 8 (flux $\mathbf q = -\nabla u$ as a second unknown, both discretised with discontinuous polynomials of degree $k$) and add a third unknown: the numerical trace $\hat u_h = \lambda_h$, a polynomial of degree $k$ on every edge. This is the hybridizable DG (HDG, or LDG-H) method of Cockburn, Gopalakrishnan and Lazarov. On every triangle $K$, for all test functions $\mathbf r\in P_k(K)^2$, $w\in P_k(K)$:

$$\begin{aligned} (\mathbf q_h,\mathbf r)_K - (u_h,\nabla\cdot\mathbf r)_K + \langle\lambda_h,\mathbf r\cdot\n\rangle_{\partial K} &= 0,\\ -(\mathbf q_h,\nabla w)_K + \langle\hat{\mathbf q}_h\cdot\n, w\rangle_{\partial K} &= (f, w)_K,\\ \hat{\mathbf q}_h\cdot\n &= \mathbf q_h\cdot\n + \tau\,(u_h - \lambda_h)\quad\text{on }\partial K, \end{aligned}$$

and on every interior edge, for all $\mu\in P_k(E)$, the transmission condition $\sum_{K\ni E}\langle\hat{\mathbf q}_h\cdot\n_K,\mu\rangle_E = 0$ (the numerical flux is single-valued: conservation). On boundary edges $\lambda_h$ is the $L^2$ projection of $g$.

The sign of the stabilisation. With our convention $\mathbf q = -\nabla u$ (heat flux), $\mathbf q\cdot\n$ is the outflow. If $u_h$ on the element boundary is higher than the trace $\lambda_h$, the term $\tau(u_h - \lambda_h) > 0$ adds outflow: heat leaves the hot element — a restoring, dissipative effect, which is why $\tau > 0$. (With the opposite convention $\mathbf q = +\nabla u$ the sign flips.) For $\tau = O(1)$ the method is stable for every $k$.

Local solvers and the global system. Exactly as for RT0: per element the first two equations form a small system ($3\dim P_k$ unknowns: $q_x, q_y, u$) with the edge traces as data; eliminating it and inserting into the transmission condition gives a global system for the trace coefficients only, again symmetric positive definite (verified in our tests for $k = 0,1,2$, $\tau = 1, 10$). Solving the condensed system and recovering element by element gives exactly the same answer as solving the large monolithic system.

Superconvergence and post-processing. For $\tau = O(1)$ and $k\ge1$, both $u_h$ and $\mathbf q_h$ converge with order $k+1$ — the flux is as accurate as the solution, unlike CG or IP-DG where the gradient loses one order. Moreover a cheap element-wise post-processing extracts one more order for $u$: find $u_h^\star\in P_{k+1}(K)$ with

$$(\nabla u_h^\star, \nabla w)_K = -(\mathbf q_h, \nabla w)_K\quad\forall w\in P_{k+1}(K),\qquad \int_K u_h^\star = \int_K u_h .$$

(The minus sign because $\mathbf q_h\approx-\nabla u$; the second condition fixes the constant, which the first cannot see.) Then $\|u - u_h^\star\| = O(h^{k+2})$. For $k = 0$ there is no such gain: everything converges with order 1.

What goes wrong
  • Edge orientation. Both triangles see a shared edge, but traverse it in opposite directions (both are counter-clockwise). An edge polynomial written in the parameter $s$ of $K^-$ must be evaluated at $1-s$ from $K^+$; forgetting this silently couples the wrong points for $k\ge1$ (for $k=0$ nothing happens, so low-order tests do not catch it).
  • Signs. The RT0 basis needs the per-element sign $s_{K,E}$; the HDG stabilisation sign depends on the flux convention. A wrong sign typically produces a perfectly plausible picture with an error that does not converge — always run a convergence test.
  • Indefinite solvers. Using CG on the saddle-point system of the mixed method fails (it is not positive definite). Hybridize first, or use a solver for indefinite systems.

Counting the cost: CG vs SIPG vs HDG

How large is the system that is finally solved? On an $N\times N$ grid ($2N^2$ triangles, about $3N^2$ interior edges) with degree $p$: CG has about $p^2N^2$ unknowns, SIPG $N^2(p+1)(p+2)$, HDG $(p+1)$ per interior edge, about $3(p+1)N^2$. HDG's count grows only linearly in $p$ because all interior-of-element unknowns were condensed away; it has fewer unknowns than SIPG for every $p$ and fewer than CG from $p = 4$ on (on our meshes, fewer non-zeros from $p = 5$ on). Two caveats keep the comparison honest: CG can also condense its element-interior (bubble) unknowns, and the cost of the local solves grows with $p$ (Kirby, Sherwin & Cockburn compare them in detail).

Summary

  • Mixed form $\bsigma = -\nabla u$, $\nabla\cdot\bsigma = f$: the flux is an unknown, Dirichlet data become natural, and $u$ is the Lagrange multiplier of conservation.
  • The discrete problem is a symmetric indefinite saddle point; it is stable only if the spaces satisfy the inf–sup condition. RT0–P0 does.
  • RT0: one unknown per edge = normal flux; basis $\pm\frac{|E|}{2|K|}(\mathbf x - \mathbf x_E)$; normal continuity by construction; exact conservation in every triangle.
  • Hybridization: break normal continuity, enforce it with multipliers $\lambda$ on the skeleton; $\lambda$ approximates $u$ on edges; static condensation (Schur complement, element by element) leaves an SPD system on the edges only.
  • HDG: hybridized DG with numerical trace $\hat u = \lambda$ and flux $\hat{\mathbf q}\cdot\n = \mathbf q\cdot\n + \tau(u - \lambda)$; order $k+1$ for $u$ and $\mathbf q$, $k+2$ after post-processing; the smallest global system among the DG methods.
