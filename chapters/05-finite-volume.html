5Finite volume methods

Store cell averages, let neighbouring cells exchange fluxes, solve little Riemann problems at the faces — and learn why the result must be nonlinear to be both sharp and wiggle-free.

Chapter 1 showed that the integral form of a conservation law is the physical one, and that its solutions can develop shocks. A finite volume (FV) method discretises exactly that integral form: the domain is cut into cells, and the unknowns are the amounts of the conserved quantity in each cell. Whatever leaves a cell through a face enters its neighbour, so the scheme conserves mass by construction — even across shocks. Everything then hinges on one question: what is the flux through a face, given only the cell averages? Answering it well is the art of numerical fluxes, and it carries over verbatim to discontinuous Galerkin methods in chapter 6.

Cell averages and the exact finite volume update

Cut the interval into cells $C_i = [x_{i-\frac12}, x_{i+\frac12}]$ of width $h$. Instead of point values, an FV method stores one number per cell, the cell average

$$\bar u_i(t) = \frac1h\int_{x_{i-\frac12}}^{x_{i+\frac12}} u(x,t)\,\mathrm dx .$$

Now apply the integral conservation law of chapter 1 to the control volume $V = C_i$. In 1D the "boundary" of $C_i$ consists of its two end points; the outward normal is $-1$ at the left face and $+1$ at the right face, so the boundary integral is just a difference:

$$\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int_{C_i} u\,\mathrm dx = -\Big[f\big(u(x_{i+\frac12},t)\big)\cdot(+1) + f\big(u(x_{i-\frac12},t)\big)\cdot(-1)\Big].$$

Divide by $h$:

$$\frac{\mathrm d\bar u_i}{\mathrm dt} = -\frac{F_{i+\frac12}(t) - F_{i-\frac12}(t)}{h},\qquad F_{i+\frac12}(t) = f\big(u(x_{i+\frac12}, t)\big).$$

This is exact — no approximation has been made yet. Integrating also over one time step $[t^n, t^{n+1}]$ gives the fully discrete, still exact, version $\bar u_i^{n+1} = \bar u_i^n - \frac{\Delta t}{h}\big(\bar F_{i+\frac12} - \bar F_{i-\frac12}\big)$ with time-averaged face fluxes $\bar F_{i+\frac12} = \frac1{\Delta t}\int_{t^n}^{t^{n+1}} f(u(x_{i+\frac12},t))\,\mathrm dt$.

The only thing we do not know is the face flux: it needs the solution at the face, while we only store averages. An FV method replaces $F_{i+\frac12}$ by a numerical flux $\hat F_{i+\frac12} = \hat F(u^-_{i+\frac12}, u^+_{i+\frac12})$ computed from the states on the two sides of the face. Whatever that choice is, the scheme is conservative: the flux $\hat F_{i+\frac12}$ is subtracted from cell $i$ and added to cell $i+1$, so summing the update over all cells the interior fluxes cancel in pairs (the sum telescopes) and

$$\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\sum_i h\,\bar u_i = \hat F_{\text{left boundary}} - \hat F_{\text{right boundary}} \qquad (= 0 \text{ for periodic boundaries}).$$

This discrete conservation is what makes FV methods compute shocks at the right speed: by the Lax–Wendroff theorem, if a conservative, consistent scheme converges, it converges to a weak solution, so shocks automatically satisfy the Rankine–Hugoniot condition of chapter 1.

Graphics analogy

A cell average is a box-filtered pixel: the value of a pixel in a properly anti-aliased image is the average of the scene over the pixel's footprint, not a point sample. "Reconstruction" below is exactly texture magnification: nearest-neighbour (piecewise constant) or linear interpolation. And the limiters we will meet play the role of the clamping used to avoid the ringing/halo artefacts of sharp reconstruction filters (Lanczos, bicubic) near edges.

Godunov's idea: reconstruct, evolve, average

Godunov (1959) turned the exact update into a method with a three-step recipe, called REA:

  1. Reconstruct a function $\tilde u(x, t^n)$ from the averages; Godunov used the piecewise constant function $\tilde u = \bar u_i^n$ on $C_i$.
  2. Evolve $\tilde u$ exactly by the PDE for one time step. Because $\tilde u$ is piecewise constant, this is a collection of Riemann problems, one at each face — as long as the waves of neighbouring faces do not interact during the step.
  3. Average the evolved function over each cell to get $\bar u_i^{n+1}$.

Since the evolution is exact, step 3 is the exact update above with $\bar F_{i+\frac12}$ computed from the Riemann solution at the face. A Riemann solution is self-similar (constant along rays $x/t = \text{const}$), so the state on the face itself, the ray $x/t = 0$, does not change during the step, and

$$\hat F^{\text{God}}(u^-, u^+) = f\big(u^*(0;\,u^-, u^+)\big),$$

where $u^*(\xi; u^-, u^+)$ is the Riemann solution at $\xi = (x - x_{i+\frac12})/(t - t^n)$. That is the Godunov flux. For linear advection $u_t + a u_x = 0$ with $a > 0$ the Riemann solution simply moves the jump to the right, so the face sees the left state: $\hat F = a\,\bar u_{i}$ at face $x_{i+\frac12}$ — this is the upwind flux, and the update becomes

$$\bar u_i^{n+1} = \bar u_i^n - \nu\,\big(\bar u_i^n - \bar u_{i-1}^n\big) = (1-\nu)\,\bar u_i^n + \nu\,\bar u_{i-1}^n,\qquad \nu = \frac{a\Delta t}{h}.$$

Geometrically: during one step a slab of width $a\Delta t$ (a fraction $\nu$ of each cell) slides into the next cell, and the new average mixes the two values with weights $1-\nu$ and $\nu$. Step through it below.

With a linear reconstruction $\tilde u = \bar u_i + \sigma_i (x - x_i)/h$ the slab crossing face $x_{i+\frac12}$ is a trapezoid of width $a\Delta t$, centred at $x_{i+\frac12} - a\Delta t/2$; its area is $a\Delta t\,\big(\bar u_i + \tfrac12(1-\nu)\sigma_i\big)$. Hence

$$\bar u_i^{n+1} = \bar u_i^n - \nu\big(\bar u_i^n - \bar u_{i-1}^n\big) - \frac{\nu(1-\nu)}{2}\big(\sigma_i - \sigma_{i-1}\big).$$

Choosing $\sigma_i = \bar u_{i+1} - \bar u_i$ gives the Lax–Wendroff scheme, $\sigma_i = \bar u_i - \bar u_{i-1}$ the Beam–Warming scheme, and the central slope $\sigma_i = \tfrac12(\bar u_{i+1} - \bar u_{i-1})$ Fromm's scheme — all second order, and all oscillating at jumps (try the unlimited slope in the widget with a large $\nu$). Remember this: we will come back to it with Godunov's theorem.

Numerical fluxes for scalar conservation laws

For nonlinear $f$, solving the Riemann problem at every face can be expensive (for systems it requires an iterative solver), so many cheaper approximate fluxes exist. Any numerical flux must be

  • consistent: $\hat F(u, u) = f(u)$ — two equal states give the physical flux;
  • Lipschitz continuous in both arguments (needed for convergence proofs);
  • and, for stability, preferably monotone: non-decreasing in $u^-$ and non-increasing in $u^+$. Writing the explicit Euler update as $\bar u_i^{n+1} = H(\bar u_{i-1}^n, \bar u_i^n, \bar u_{i+1}^n)$, a monotone flux makes $H$ non-decreasing in each argument under a CFL condition: the scheme is monotone — it cannot create new maxima or minima.

The classic choices, with $u^- = u_L$, $u^+ = u_R$:

All of them except "central" have the form central flux minus a dissipation term $\tfrac12 Q\,(u_R - u_L)$ with a coefficient $Q\ge 0$: upwind/Roe use $Q = |\hat a|$, Rusanov uses $Q = \alpha \ge |\hat a|$ (more dissipative). The dissipation term is what damps the instability of the central flux.

Roe and expansion shocks. Roe's flux treats every jump as a single wave of speed $\hat a$. For Burgers' equation with $u_L = -1 < 0 < u_R = 1$ the true solution is a rarefaction fan spreading in both directions, but $\hat a = (u_L + u_R)/2 = 0$, so Roe's flux is $\tfrac12 f(u_L) + \tfrac12 f(u_R) = \tfrac12$, which is exactly $f(u_L) = f(u_R)$: the jump produces no change at all and stays as a stationary, entropy-violating expansion shock forever. Harten's entropy fix replaces $|\hat a|$ by $(\hat a^2 + \delta^2)/(2\delta)$ whenever $|\hat a| < \delta$: near sonic points (where $f'$ changes sign) a little dissipation is always added, and the fan opens. The CFL condition

For the explicit upwind scheme, $\bar u_i^{n+1}$ depends on $\bar u_i^n$ and $\bar u_{i-1}^n$; after $n$ steps it depends on cells $i-n,\dots,i$ — the numerical domain of dependence is the interval $[x_i - n h, x_i]$. The exact solution at $(x_i, t^n)$ is $u_0(x_i - a\,n\Delta t)$: its physical domain of dependence is the single point where the characteristic through $(x_i, t^n)$ started. Courant, Friedrichs and Lewy (1928) observed that a convergent scheme must "see" that point: if $a\,n\Delta t > n h$, the information that determines the solution never reaches the scheme, and changing $u_0$ there would change the exact solution but not the numerical one. Hence the CFL condition

$$\nu = \frac{|a|\,\Delta t}{h} \le 1 \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \Delta t \le \frac{h}{|a|}\qquad\text{(nonlinear: } |a| \to \max_i |f'(\bar u_i)|).$$

The CFL condition is necessary; for upwind it is also sufficient. Look at the update $\bar u_i^{n+1} = (1-\nu)\bar u_i^n + \nu\,\bar u_{i-1}^n$: for $0\le\nu\le1$ it is a convex combination, so $\min \bar u^n \le \bar u^{n+1}_i \le \max \bar u^n$ (a discrete maximum principle — nothing can grow). For $\nu > 1$ the weight $1-\nu$ is negative. A Fourier mode $\bar u_j^n = g^n e^{\mathrm i j\theta}$ is multiplied per step by $g(\theta) = 1 - \nu + \nu e^{-\mathrm i\theta}$, with

$$|g(\theta)|^2 = 1 - 2\nu(1-\nu)(1 - \cos\theta).$$

For $0 \le \nu \le 1$ we get $|g| \le 1$ for all $\theta$; for $\nu > 1$ the product $\nu(1-\nu)$ is negative and the sawtooth mode $\theta = \pi$ grows like $|1 - 2\nu|^n$: at $\nu = 1.2$ by a factor $1.4$ per step. At exactly $\nu = 1$ the update is $\bar u_i^{n+1} = \bar u_{i-1}^n$: the exact solution, shifted by one cell.

  • Burgers, Riemann B (a transonic fan $-1 \to 1$), first order, Roe flux: a frozen expansion shock. Switch to "Roe + Harten entropy fix", Godunov or Rusanov.
  • Buckley–Leverett, Riemann A: the solution is a compound wave — a rarefaction immediately followed by a shock — because the flux is not convex.
  • MUSCL with limiter "none" on the square pulse: second order, but over- and undershoots appear. Now try minmod and superbee.
What goes wrong

The CFL number is computed from the current maximal wave speed. For nonlinear problems that speed can grow during a step (or between the stages of a Runge–Kutta method), and for systems you must use the fastest wave speed $|u| + c$, not the flow speed $|u|$. Using a fixed $\Delta t$ chosen from the initial data is a classic source of mysterious blow-ups.

Numerical diffusion: the modified equation

Why does the first-order upwind scheme smear the square pulse? Ask which PDE the scheme solves more accurately than the one it was designed for. Insert a smooth function $u(x,t)$ into the scheme $\frac{u(x,t+\Delta t) - u(x,t)}{\Delta t} + a\,\frac{u(x,t) - u(x-h,t)}{h} = 0$ and Taylor-expand:

$$u_t + \tfrac12\Delta t\,u_{tt} + \mathcal O(\Delta t^2) + a\big(u_x - \tfrac12 h\,u_{xx} + \mathcal O(h^2)\big) = 0 .$$

To leading order $u_t = -a u_x$, so $u_{tt} = -a u_{xt} = a^2 u_{xx}$. Substituting,

$$u_t + a\,u_x = \frac{a h}{2}\big(1 - \nu\big)\,u_{xx} + \mathcal O(h^2),\qquad \nu = \frac{a\Delta t}{h}.$$

The upwind scheme is a second-order accurate discretisation of an advection–diffusion equation with numerical diffusivity $D = \frac{a h}{2}(1-\nu)$ (Warming & Hyett 1974). The diffusion vanishes for $\nu = 1$ (exact shift) and becomes negative — anti-diffusion, unstable — for $\nu > 1$: the CFL condition again.

This is the precise sense in which "upwinding adds artificial viscosity". Since $D \propto h$, a jump is smeared over a width $\sim\sqrt{D t}\sim\sqrt{h}$, which is why the $L^1$ error of upwind for a discontinuity decreases only like $h^{1/2}$, not $h$.

Godunov's order barrier

First order is too diffusive; Lax–Wendroff and friends are second order but oscillate. Is there a linear scheme that is both second order and free of oscillations? Godunov proved in 1959 that there is not.

Theorem (Godunov). A linear scheme $\bar u_i^{n+1} = \sum_k c_k\,\bar u^n_{i+k}$ for $u_t + a u_x = 0$ that is monotone (all $c_k \ge 0$, so it never creates new extrema) is at most first-order accurate.

Proof (it is short and pretty). Second-order accuracy means the scheme is exact for quadratic polynomials: inserting $u = 1$, $u = x$ and $u = x^2$ and comparing with the exact shift $u(x - a\Delta t)$ gives (with $\nu = a\Delta t/h$)

$$\sum_k c_k = 1,\qquad \sum_k k\,c_k = -\nu,\qquad \sum_k k^2 c_k = \nu^2 .$$

If all $c_k \ge 0$, these are the moments of a probability distribution on the integers $k$ with mean $-\nu$ and second moment $\nu^2$. Its variance is $\sum_k k^2 c_k - (\sum_k k c_k)^2 = \nu^2 - \nu^2 = 0$, so all weight sits at a single integer $k = -\nu$: the scheme is the trivial exact shift for integer $\nu$. For any other $\nu$, no such scheme exists. $\square$

The way out is to give up linearity: let the scheme look at the data and decide locally — high order where the solution is smooth, first order near jumps. Such schemes are called high-resolution schemes, and the device that decides is a limiter.

High resolution: MUSCL reconstruction and slope limiters

Van Leer's MUSCL approach ("Monotone Upstream-centred Scheme for Conservation Laws", 1979) keeps Godunov's framework but reconstructs a piecewise linear function in each cell, with a slope chosen nonlinearly from the neighbouring averages:

$$\tilde u(x) = \bar u_i + \sigma_i\,\frac{x - x_i}{h}\ \ \text{on } C_i,\qquad u^-_{i+\frac12} = \bar u_i + \tfrac12\sigma_i,\qquad u^+_{i-\frac12} = \bar u_i - \tfrac12\sigma_i .$$

The face values are then fed into any numerical flux. With the two one-sided differences $\Delta_- = \bar u_i - \bar u_{i-1}$ and $\Delta_+ = \bar u_{i+1} - \bar u_i$ the classic limiters are

limiterslope $\sigma_i$ (zero if $\Delta_-\Delta_+ \le 0$)$\varphi(r)$, $\ r = \Delta_-/\Delta_+$
minmodthe one of $\Delta_-, \Delta_+$ with smaller modulus$\max(0, \min(1, r))$
monotonised central (MC)$\operatorname{sign}(\Delta_+)\min\big(2|\Delta_-|,\ 2|\Delta_+|,\ \tfrac12|\Delta_- + \Delta_+|\big)$$\max\big(0,\min(2r, \tfrac{1+r}2, 2)\big)$
van Leer$\dfrac{2\Delta_-\Delta_+}{\Delta_- + \Delta_+}$ (harmonic mean)$\dfrac{r + |r|}{1 + |r|}$
superbee$\operatorname{sign}(\Delta_+)\max\big(\min(2|\Delta_-|, |\Delta_+|),\ \min(|\Delta_-|, 2|\Delta_+|)\big)$$\max\big(0, \min(2r, 1), \min(r, 2)\big)$

In all cases $\sigma_i = \varphi(r_i)\,\Delta_+$. At a local extremum ($\Delta_-\Delta_+ \le 0$, i.e. $r \le 0$) the slope is set to zero: the scheme drops to first order there, so it cannot overshoot. In smooth monotone regions $r \approx 1$ and $\varphi(1) = 1$: the slope is the correct second-order one.

Total variation diminishing (TVD) schemes and Sweby's diagram

What does "no oscillations" mean mathematically? Harten (1983) proposed: the total variation $\mathrm{TV}(\bar u) = \sum_i |\bar u_{i+1} - \bar u_i|$ must not increase, $\mathrm{TV}(\bar u^{n+1}) \le \mathrm{TV}(\bar u^n)$. A new wiggle always adds variation, so a TVD scheme cannot create one. Harten's lemma: a scheme that can be written as $\bar u_i^{n+1} = \bar u_i^n - C_{i-\frac12}(\bar u_i^n - \bar u_{i-1}^n)$ with $0 \le C_{i-\frac12} \le 1$ is TVD. For MUSCL with the upwind flux ($a > 0$) and forward Euler, $C_{i-\frac12} = \nu\big[1 + \tfrac12\varphi(r_i)/r_i - \tfrac12\varphi(r_{i-1})\big]$. If

$$\varphi(r) = 0 \text{ for } r \le 0,\qquad 0 \le \varphi(r) \le \min(2r,\, 2) \text{ for } r > 0,$$

then the bracket lies in $[0, 2]$ and the scheme is TVD for $\nu \le \tfrac12$. This is Sweby's TVD region (Sweby 1984; the same region appears in the flux-limiter form of Lax–Wendroff, where the CFL limit is $\nu\le1$). Second-order accuracy additionally needs $\varphi(1) = 1$ and $\varphi$ smooth near $r = 1$; Sweby recommends limiters between minmod (the lower boundary) and superbee (the upper boundary). Fromm's central slope, $\varphi = (1+r)/2$, leaves the region — which is why it oscillates.

What order do we really get?

A limiter clips the slope at smooth extrema, so a TVD scheme is formally only first order at extrema; in the $L^1$ norm these few cells hardly matter and the observed rate on smooth data stays close to 2. For discontinuous solutions no scheme converges fast: the measured $L^1$ rates for a transported jump are about $\tfrac12$ (first order) and about $\tfrac23$ (MUSCL with minmod, van Leer, MC), because the smearing grows like $h^{1/2}$ and $h^{2/3}$, respectively. Nonlinear problems behave better at shocks: characteristics run into a shock and keep it sharp, so first-order schemes reach rate $\approx 1$ at shocks, but only $\approx 0.75$ at rarefactions at practical resolutions (corners at the fan edges; this is verified in tests/conv/fv1d.test.js). Time stepping: strong-stability-preserving Runge–Kutta

MUSCL as written above is a semi-discrete scheme, $\frac{\mathrm d\bar u}{\mathrm dt} = L(\bar u)$, an ODE system — the "method of lines". Forward Euler is only first order in time, so we need a higher-order ODE solver that does not destroy the TVD property. The SSP Runge–Kutta methods of Shu and Osher (1988) do this: they are convex combinations of forward-Euler steps,

$$\begin{aligned} \text{SSP-RK2:}\quad & u^{(1)} = u^n + \Delta t\,L(u^n), \quad u^{n+1} = \tfrac12 u^n + \tfrac12\big(u^{(1)} + \Delta t\,L(u^{(1)})\big),\\ \text{SSP-RK3:}\quad & u^{(1)} = u^n + \Delta t\,L(u^n),\quad u^{(2)} = \tfrac34 u^n + \tfrac14\big(u^{(1)} + \Delta t\,L(u^{(1)})\big),\\ & u^{n+1} = \tfrac13 u^n + \tfrac23\big(u^{(2)} + \Delta t\,L(u^{(2)})\big). \end{aligned}$$

If one forward-Euler step is TVD for $\Delta t \le \Delta t_{\text{FE}}$, every stage is TVD and so is any convex combination of them (the TV is a convex function): the whole RK step is TVD under the same time-step limit. The implementation in this course is lib/core/time/rk.js (makeStepper('ssprk3', n)). In the tests, MUSCL with any of the four limiters and forward Euler, SSP-RK2 or SSP-RK3 never increased the total variation at $\nu = 0.45$.

What goes wrong

The classical RK4 is not SSP: it cannot be written as a convex combination of forward-Euler steps (no four-stage fourth-order method can; Gottlieb & Shu 1998), so the argument above does not apply and, combined with a limiter, small oscillations can appear that forward Euler would not produce. Also, "TVD" says nothing about systems: for the Euler equations no TVD theory exists, and limiting primitive or characteristic variables is a well-tested heuristic, not a theorem.

Systems: the Euler equations

For gas dynamics (chapter 1) the unknowns are $\mathbf U = (\rho, \rho u, E)$ with flux $\mathbf F(\mathbf U) = (\rho u,\ \rho u^2 + p,\ u(E + p))$ and the ideal-gas law $p = (\gamma - 1)(E - \tfrac12\rho u^2)$, $\gamma = 1.4$. The FV update is literally the same, $\frac{\mathrm d\bar{\mathbf U}_i}{\mathrm dt} = -(\hat{\mathbf F}_{i+\frac12} - \hat{\mathbf F}_{i-\frac12})/h$, applied component-wise. The new ingredient is the Riemann problem. The flux Jacobian $\partial\mathbf F/\partial\mathbf U$ has the three eigenvalues

$$\lambda_1 = u - c,\qquad \lambda_2 = u,\qquad \lambda_3 = u + c,\qquad c = \sqrt{\gamma p/\rho},$$

so the solution of a Riemann problem consists of three waves separating four constant states: a left acoustic wave (a shock or a rarefaction fan), a contact discontinuity moving with the fluid (density jumps, but velocity and pressure do not) and a right acoustic wave. In Sod's shock tube $(\rho, u, p) = (1, 0, 1)$ for $x < \tfrac12$ and $(0.125, 0, 0.1)$ for $x > \tfrac12$ — a membrane separating high- from low-pressure gas bursts — the left wave is a rarefaction, the right wave a shock.

The exact Riemann solver (Toro 2009, chapter 4) exploits that $u$ and $p$ are continuous across the contact. Let $p^*$ be the pressure between the two acoustic waves. Across the left wave, the velocity change is a known function of $p^*$ — from the Rankine–Hugoniot conditions if $p^* > p_L$ (shock), from the isentropic relations if $p^* \le p_L$ (rarefaction) — and likewise on the right. Equating the two velocities gives one scalar equation $f_L(p^*) + f_R(p^*) + u_R - u_L = 0$, solved by Newton's method in a handful of iterations. For Sod: $p^* \approx 0.30313$, $u^* \approx 0.92745$.

Approximate Riemann solvers replace the exact fan by something cheaper:

  • Rusanov: $\hat{\mathbf F} = \tfrac12(\mathbf F_L + \mathbf F_R) - \tfrac12 S^+(\mathbf U_R - \mathbf U_L)$ with $S^+ = \max(|u_L| + c_L, |u_R| + c_R)$: one speed for all waves. Robust, diffusive.
  • HLL (Harten, Lax & van Leer 1983): one averaged state between the slowest and fastest signal speeds $S_L, S_R$; $\hat{\mathbf F} = \frac{S_R\mathbf F_L - S_L\mathbf F_R + S_L S_R(\mathbf U_R - \mathbf U_L)}{S_R - S_L}$ when $S_L < 0 < S_R$. With Einfeldt's speed estimates it keeps density and pressure positive. It ignores the contact, which gets smeared.
  • HLLC (Toro, Spruce & Speares 1994): HLL with the Contact restored — two intermediate states separated by a middle wave of speed $S^*$. Resolves an isolated stationary contact exactly.
  • Roe (1981): solves the linearised problem $\mathbf U_t + \tilde A\,\mathbf U_x = 0$ exactly, with a cleverly averaged matrix $\tilde A(\mathbf U_L, \mathbf U_R)$: $\hat{\mathbf F} = \tfrac12(\mathbf F_L + \mathbf F_R) - \tfrac12\sum_k |\tilde\lambda_k|\,\tilde\alpha_k\,\tilde{\mathbf r}_k$ (eigenvalues, wave strengths and eigenvectors of $\tilde A$). Sharp — it resolves isolated shocks and contacts exactly — but it needs an entropy fix at sonic rarefactions, and it can produce negative pressures near vacuum (try the 123 problem).

Two dimensions on Cartesian grids

On a grid of rectangles $C_{ij}$ of size $h_x\times h_y$, the integral form over a cell gives four face integrals instead of two point values:

$$\frac{\mathrm d\bar u_{ij}}{\mathrm dt} = -\frac{F_{i+\frac12,j} - F_{i-\frac12,j}}{h_x} - \frac{G_{i,j+\frac12} - G_{i,j-\frac12}}{h_y},$$

where $F_{i+\frac12,j}$ is the average of the normal flux $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf e_x$ over the vertical face and $G$ the same for horizontal faces — exactly the divergence theorem from chapter 0 applied to a rectangle. The simplest second-order approximation evaluates the face average at the face midpoint (midpoint rule) with a 1D numerical flux in the normal direction; the reconstruction is done dimension by dimension, with a limited slope in $x$ for the vertical faces and a limited slope in $y$ for the horizontal faces. For linear transport $u_t + \nabla\cdot(\mathbf a u) = 0$ the upwind flux at a face with normal velocity $a_n$ is $a_n u^-$ if $a_n \ge 0$ and $a_n u^+$ otherwise. The CFL condition of this "unsplit" first-order scheme is $\Delta t\,\big(|a_x|/h_x + |a_y|/h_y\big) \le 1$ — in 2D the time step is (up to) twice as small as in 1D, because a cell loses material through two faces at once.

The classic test is Zalesak's slotted disk (1979) in a solid-body rotation $\mathbf a = 2\pi(-(y - \tfrac12),\ x - \tfrac12)$: after one revolution the exact solution equals the initial one, so every difference is numerical error. The slot is only five cells wide at $100\times100$ resolution — a demanding test of numerical diffusion.

What goes wrong

The midpoint rule on faces limits the dimension-by-dimension scheme to second order no matter how accurate the reconstruction is: for higher order (WENO, DG) you need several quadrature points per face. And splitting the 2D problem into alternating 1D sweeps (Strang splitting) is a different scheme with its own (directional) error — both are used in practice.

Summary

  • FV methods evolve cell averages by the exact relation $\mathrm d\bar u_i/\mathrm dt = -(F_{i+\frac12} - F_{i-\frac12})/h$; the only approximation is the numerical flux. Conservation holds by construction.
  • Godunov's REA: reconstruct, evolve exactly (Riemann problems at faces), average. The Godunov flux is $f$ of the Riemann solution at the face; upwind, Rusanov, Roe, HLL, HLLC are cheaper approximations.
  • Explicit schemes need the CFL condition $\Delta t\max|f'| \le h$ (domain of dependence); upwind is a convex combination exactly when $\nu \le 1$.
  • Upwind solves $u_t + a u_x = \frac{a h}2(1-\nu) u_{xx}$ to second order: first-order schemes are diffusive.
  • Godunov's theorem: linear monotone schemes are at most first order. High resolution requires nonlinear limiters; Sweby's region $0\le\varphi\le\min(2r,2)$ guarantees TVD, SSP-RK keeps it in time.
  • Systems need wave-aware fluxes (HLLC, Roe, exact) for sharp contacts, and positivity-preserving ones (HLL, HLLC, Rusanov) near vacuum.
  • Next chapter: instead of reconstructing a polynomial from neighbours, DG methods store the polynomial in each cell — with exactly the same numerical fluxes on faces.
